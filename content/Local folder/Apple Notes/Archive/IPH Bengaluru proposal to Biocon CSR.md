The Institute of Public Health Bengaluru (IPH Bengaluru) is a non-profit public health academic institution established in 2005, with a vision of creating an equitable, integrated, decentralised and participatory health system within a just and empowered society. IPH Bengaluru has been recognized by the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (DSIR), Ministry of Science and Technology, Government of India as a Scientific and Research Organization (SIRO). We are also a partner in the National Corporate Social (CSR) Responsibility Hub at Tata Institute of Social Sciences, Mumbai and are registered with NITI Aayog (DARPAN ID KA/2016/0105575). We have a long track-record of working with national and state governments, international agencies such as WHO and UNICEF, with reputed NGOs and  CSR partners. IPH Bengaluru has been accredited by the Financial Management Service Foundation with a Platinum rating in recognition of good practices in the areas of financial management, governance, Internal control systems, and legal compliance. IPH Bengaluru head office is in Bangalore and has established a long-term public health research and action field station in BR Hills, Chamrajanagar district, Karnataka. 


The BR Hills field station of IPH Bengaluru steers district level and community-based engagement on tribal health, non-communicable diseases (including mental health), zoonotic diseases and neglected health problems such as sickle cell disease. Since 2014, IPH Bengaluru has expanded its work with the Soliga and Jens Kuruba tribal communities and works in close partnership with community-based organisation of the Soliga, Jens Kuruba and other tribal communities in the district, as well as with local NGOs, particularly, the Vivekananda Girijana Kalyana Kendra. IPH Bengaluru has long-term collaboration MoUs with VGKK and the community-based organisations of the Soliga community to take up locally relevant research and development initiatives. 

Over the last several decades, our research has demonstrated the increase of non-communicable diseases especially Diabetes Mellitus among tribal communities. It was earlier assumed that tribal communities may have lesser incidence of NCDs such as Diabetes, Hypertension, cancer and mental health problems due to relatively healthier environment and food. However, research over the last decades in multiple tribal areas in the country by ICMR as well as several other research organisations have shown the increasing incidence of NCDs, particularly Diabetes, Hypertension and stroke. At the same time, the limited availabity of specialist/secondary/tertiary care services in remote rural areas and in relatively poor HDI districts such as Chamarajangar have increased the challenges related to mental health problems as well as cancer. Cancer, stroke and various NCDs are often detected very late among tribal communities and these often require expensive healthcare in distant hospitals in Bangalore/urban centres and they often hesitate or neglect these resulting in increased morbidity and mortality due to NCDs among tribal communities. 

In the current project, IPH Bengaluru aims to establish a collaborative partnership with local NGOs and the community-based organisations of the Soliga and other tribal communities in Chamaraanagar district in order to:
- Create a community-based early detection and screening centre for NCDs among tribal communities of CHamarajangar district
- Design and implement an outreach programme to improve referral and care for NCDs among tribal communities
- Build a long-term model of care for NCDs that can be scaled up to other tribal populations in partnership with local NGOs & CBOs. 


We will begin with setting up a hub at the Vivekananda Tribal Health Centre (managed by VGKK hospital with >40 year track-record of trial health care). The hub hospital will have facilities including a doctor trained in NCDs, a laboratory upgraded for NCD case detection and health records system for long-term/continous care for NCDs. Hub will also have linkages with secondary/tertiary facilities as needed to organise tele-health and in-person referral for cases detected. 

One model envisions the *spokes* (in the hub-and-spoke model) to be constituted of three motorcycle-based mobile units with one male & female trained tribal health workers. Each unit will have a catchment population consisting of a cluster of hamlets. They shall have a calendar of visits to these hamelets and will organise awareness, household screening, surveys and community engagement on  NCDs. They will also be the liaison with the government AHSA health workers and the representatives of the Soliga Adivasi Sangha members. Referral from them will be done to the hub hospital.  The pros of this model is the improved access during rainy season in forest areas where there are no *pucca* roads. However, the cons include the difficulty of organising large footfall camps, challenges of patient transport and travel during heavy rains (3-4 months every year). 

An alternate model is to have a 4-wheeler mobile health unit which overcomes the limitations of the aforementioned model. This mobile health unit could be in addition to the motorcycle-based unit described above or could be instead of (depending on the resources available). The functionality of this unit will be the same as above. However, it will be additinally able to support need-based travel of doctors/lab-techs as well as enable all-weather access to all settlements. Recurring costs will be correspondingly higher than motorcycle-units. 

This kind of an NCD care model for tribal communities has not been done due to resource limitations and remoteness of tribal areas. This could be a model for expanding NCD care to tribal populations. 

Direct impact
- Adults >30 years of age among tribal communities in and around BRT Tiger Reserve estimated to be about 3500-4000 beneficiaries over the year

Indirect impact
- 30 Soliga tribal settlements in and around BRT Tiger Reserve consisting of about 8000 population
