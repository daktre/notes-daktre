After my medicine, 



Being trained as a doctor, my professional career began as a doctor in primary health care at an NGO in southern Karnataka. My love for wildlife and forests drew me to work in challenging settings in forest areas of southern Karnataka and later in Arunachal Pradesh in northeast India. In all these places, I saw first-hand the challenges in organizing and managing community health facilities. Implementing disease control programmes and providing good quality health services in these areas were depending as much on evidence-based and scientifically sound medicine, as they did on addressing social, political, economic and cultural barriers to healthcare as well as the various social determinants of health. My early experience in trying to plan and manage services better in challenging work environments and in areas where the local health systems are not yet equipped in terms of governance, human resources and other infrastructure gave me a clear picture of the underlying systemic challenges to achieving good population health outcomes in India. With this learning, and my curiosity to understand what ails the system itself, I pursued a masters in public health. A scholarship enabled me to do my Masters in one of the leading centres of excellence in tropical medicine and public health, the Institute of Tropical Medicine, Antwerp, Belgium. At ITM, my thesis on primary health care was awarded a medal of merit by the city of Antwerp (for the best masters thesis award **across all disciplines**). 
Encouraged by this, I returned to India with a renewed energy for pursuing research to improve population health. My attention was shifting towards the health system itself: can the application of more rigorous and inter- disciplinary methods help us better understand why local health systems are not able to perform better? This curiosity of trying to scientifically understand reasons for poorly functioning local health systems led me to propose a PhD dissertation topic focusing on district level health system performance. I was fascinated by the concepts of complexity and systems thinking within health and was privileged to obtain a PhD fellowship from ITM, Antwerp to work with people with experience in various health policy and systems research methods. For my PhD, I used various quantitative and qualitative methods together within a larger theory-driven evaluation framework to develop an explanation for poor health system performance in response to systemic capacity- building efforts. My PhD fellowship also allowed me to pursue courses on complexity, systems thinking and on mixed methods. 
Even as my PhD was coming to a conclusion, my interest in better understanding access and the inequities therein, and the role of the health system in identifying these inequalities and addressing them increased. This was spurred by a larger focus on achieving equity in health, that today drives my research interest. Subsequently, I attended courses and specialized in the application of theory-driven inquiry within public health. I also used this for my PhD dissertation. In 2014, I was invited by the public health department of the University of Umea, Sweden to co-teach theory-driven inquiry approaches in a workshop. I have also taught the application of this approach within health systems research at similar workshops organized by KEYSTONE consortium of the Public Health Foundation of India too. 
I believe that there is very little research into describing, and more importantly, understanding and addressing inequalities in healthcare provision, utilization and in health outcomes. While the body of biomedical research slowly improves from low- and middle income countries like India, we still trail behind in generating an equally large and robust body of literature on why health and other development continues to be inequitable, despite several recent efforts at system strengthening. I believe that generating locally relevant and scientifically sound data and forging collaborations between researchers and implementers is going to be crucial in instilling a culture and practice of evidence-based policy and practice. 	

For the subsequent 2012 second global symposium on health systems research, I was invited to be on the symposium steering committee as a representative of young researchers (http://www.healthsystemsresearch.org/hsr2012/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=53&Itemid=60). In the scientific committee, my role was to participate in discussions related to ensuring peer review of the abstracts and ensuring quality standards for technical quality of presentations at the symposium. At the 2012 symposium, I made an oral presentation on the application of realist evaluation approach (one of the types of theory-driven evaluation approaches) for studying local health system performance. At the 2014 symposium, the third global symposium on health systems research, I was involved in the scientific committee and I reviewed oral, poster and organized session submissions. At the Cape Town Symposium, I proposed to facilitate participation of policymakers within such symposia for improving collaboration and dialogue between policymakers and researchers. Based on my proposition, the WHO Alliance supported the participation of the Health Minister of Karnataka at the symposium. I facilitated his participation in a panel discussion on application of systems thinking approaches to health. I was also invited as a panelist on a panel discussing the role of and scope for realist and theory-driven approaches within health systems research as a way of dealing with the complexity of achieving health systems change. 
At the upcoming Vancouver symposium later in November 2016, as the Chairperson of the Emerging Voices for Global Health Initiative and a member of the scientific committee, I am involved in designing and managing the call for young researchers to participate in this symposium. I am also invited to be on a panel that will discuss “Engaging with complexity in health policy and systems research: experience from applying three complex- sensitive approaches” with other panelists from universities in Belgium and Sweden, as well as researchers from East Africa. 
My PhD research explored the application of systems thinking within public health. I was fascinated by how healthcare organisations respond differently to the same intervention (programme or policy). In Tumkur district, I carefully followed up on hospital and health outcomes at the sub-district level, especially focusing on the differences in these outcomes and in finding an explanation for why this occurs. My scientific quest took me to working with social scientists and correspondingly the research methods that allow for investigating social phenomena. My analysis was invited as an illustrative case study for advancing the application of systems thinking in health, a special journal series that was released at the Third Global Symposium on Health Systems Research held at Cape Town, South Africa in 2014. At the symposium, I accompanied a senior policymaker from Karnataka to engage in discussions on the implications of the study for design of public health interventions for strengthening local health systems in Karnataka.

In 2010, I was chosen as one of the Emerging Voices for Global Health, a global competitive call for young researchers from the global South who are interested in global health, but also committed to local change through research. Being one of the Emerging Voices fellows, I participated in the first Global Symposium for Health Systems Research held in Montreux in November 2010. At the symposium, I made an oral presentation, making a case for health policy and systems relevant research questions, using the example of public private partnership in primary health care. My presentation was on how little public health policy leans on evidence on one hand, and how little evidence exists for crucial policy decisions on the other hand, using the example of public private partnership in primary health care. This spurred further an interest to focus on the general lack of application of many emerging social science based research methods in public health evaluation practice, resulting in a paper on what kind of research methods and research questions prevail within health policy research in India. My oral presentation at the symposium was published as a review/commentary in Economic and Political Weekly. 

For my PhD, I used a realist evaluation (a type of theory-driven inquiry) approach to understand the nature of organisational change within local health systems, in response to capacity building programmes. I used a mix of quantitative data (pychometric measurements of commitment and efficacy towards public organisations) and qualitative data (in-depth interviews and focus group discussions) in several iterations to arrive at a theoretical explanation for why some health managers respond postiively to capacity building efforts and other do not. I began with reviewing the various research methods that are being used for evaluation studies in India. I then studied a complex intervention to build capacity of health managers in Tumkur district in southern Karnataka, using a realist evaluation approach (protocol published). I collected both qualitative and quantitative data and conducted three iterations of data colletion and analyisis to refine the programme theory of the intervention to better understand what worked, for whom, under what conditions and why. The results and discussion report on the internal local helath system environment and external policy envionrment and their alignments needed for capacity building to result in district health planning, supervision and improvement of health outcomes.
