Scientific progress has been steady in various subjects and fields over the last several decades. While advancing deeper and farther, scientific work has also expanded geographically into populations and geographies where neither scientific advances nor scientific training was previously available. However, in order to be transformative (and not just descriptive, exploratory or predictive), science (and scientists) have to engage with people, governments and social institutions. This is a fundamental roadblock that is preventing scientific advances from benefitting all countries, populations and cultures equitably. 

Nutrition science is at the heart of realising human rights. In this sense, nutrition science has transformative potential. However, the benefits of nutrition science can only be realised thorugh effective health and nutrition systems that work for all (and not only for those who are able to act upon information). The work of the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health has shown that the ultimate causes of disease and ill-health lie within the social determinants, which need sustained action on ground and new approaches that involve working across disciplines, across comfort zones of laboratories, and embedded within populations and social institutions. 

I believe that action on nutrition is of fundamental importance in many of India’s forested areas, where several indigenous populations (called *Adivasi* locally) continue to have a close relationship with forests. Like everywhere else, their relationship with local natural resources and landscapes is shifting much rapidly than before. Many of India’s tribal populations are increasingly interacting with food and nutrition practices and supply chains coming in from the governments as well as through the market mechanisms. With non-communicable diseases especially Diabetes and Hypertension on the rise across India, very little is known of our tribal population. What we do know is that under-nutrition - be it among children under 5 or among adults - is worse off among India’s tribal populations when compared to any other. Advances in nutrition science must first be able to benefit them, and one of the important roadblocks here is for scientists (and science) to be able to set up a dialogue and a participatory research agenda embedded within these areas. Researchers need to engage with implementers, governments and communities in innovative ways, using implementation reseach and participatory action research to advance benefits from nutrition science to tribal communities.

I am a mid-career public health researcher at the Institute of Public Health in Bangalore (India). With the support of the Wellcome Trust/DBT India Alliance, I have set up a public health research station in a forested area with several tribal communities in southern Karnataka. While the main objective of my fellowship is to characterise and act upon drivers of inequities in this tribal area. And nutritional status of children and adult tribal households is one of the core elements of my surveys in this region. In the coming year, I will be surveying three tribal regions across the country to understand context-specific drivers of tribal health inequities. Based on the patterns of inequities, I am hoping to build explanatory theories on why these inequity patterns are brought about and co-create with the local community (of Soliga tribal people) and implementers, an equity-enhancing intervention to act on tribal health inequity. I believe that my participation will bring the perspective of a mid-career health policy & systems researcher into the meeting, especially from the viewpoint of forest-associated tribal communities in southern India. 

I am a medical doctor with a PhD in public health.
