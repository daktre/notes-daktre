1. A letter of motivation (max 2000 characters). 

I started my training as a medical doctor in the latter half of the 1990s. My journey into medicine coincided with the time India was “opening up” its markets and freeing itself from government red tape to reap the benefits of economic development. From seeing my first telephone at the fag end of my high school, I quickly transited to owning a mobile phone and assembling a desktop on my own in my government medical college hostel. It was a crazy fast forward from late 90s to mid 2000s, when I was able to rapidly benefit from global information technology revolutions in the Silicon valley that percolated so rapidly to Bangalore. While globalisation has always been around, never before had the rate of change been so rapid. 

At medical school, I learned medicine too. I remember the pride in showing off knowledge of ossification dates and trivia about eponymous syndromes. Yes, I learnt case taking and diagnostic skills as a means to an end; an end of passing examinations. Soon to discover during internship how the best of diagnostic skills would not help me connect to an injured 60-year-old farmer from a nearby tiger reserve mauled by an elephant and his crop lying in a heap of waste. It would not help me communicate death to the daughter or birth to the mother. These, I had to learn by myself as most of my colleagues did. Often did I seek the Corrigan’s secret door (a metaphorical escape route for busy physicians.). 

When I finished medicine and entered “society” as a doctor, the fascination with the rapidity of change quickly disappeared. Less than 100 kilometres from the medical college city I studied, there was not a single psychiatrist for an entire district of close to a million and this is not a very atypical district. Malnutrition and Anaemia, treatments for which are extremely well known were still claiming lives. My initiation into community health and public health began through four years of work in a nearby tribal hospital. My initial fascination with the landscape was through my fascination with birds and wildlife. But rather quickly, the inequality of the progress that I saw in bustling Indian cities struck me. Very clearly, progress was limited to specific geographical, populations and cultures. The world in entirety was not changing similarly, nor equally. After five years of clinical work in various forested areas combining my love for birds and nature with a quest for a meaningful contribution to society, I took on the path of research, not discovery or curiosity oriented research, but of trying to strengthen health systems; to embark on a journey to make a change in the way in which health and healthcare is distributed in our society, especially for the worse-off, in this case for indigenous peoples closely associated with forests. 

In late 2013, I was instrumental in establishing a new field practice area for the Institute of Public Health Bangalore (IPH) in the tribal regions of Chamarajanagar district in southern Karnataka. The purpose was to invest in the development of a granular understanding of the reasons for the lagging behind of several population groups, such as tribal communities. The field station is situated within the BR Hills forest area of the district. We use an implementation research approach to identify barriers and strengthen implementation of maternal health programmes in the district. Our work led to an invitation to write a background paper on health for the Indian government’s national human development report exclusively focusing on the tribal population.7 On the invitation of the government, for the past few years, I have also contributed to the Karnataka state annual health plan specifically for the design of interventions to improve tribal health. Subsequently, I have been involved with synthesising research on health inequalities in India, especially focussing on understanding how and why there are unfair differences between various socio-economic population groups across India (in collaboration with the proposed mentor). In the current proposal, we seek to build upon these preliminary insights from the field and from literature on tribal health in India.
