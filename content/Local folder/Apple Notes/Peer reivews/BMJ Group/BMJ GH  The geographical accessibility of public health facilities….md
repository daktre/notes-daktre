Thank you for the opportunity to review this paper. The paper uses a recent dataset of public health facilities in Nepal and assesses the inequalities with respect to geographical accessibility of health facilities at a relatively fine scale (1 sq. Km) by walk and by motorised transport. The novelty is in creating fine-scale maps that enable evidence-based assessment of under-served areas and hence the work is of benefit in addressing inequities. That said, a major shortcoming of the paper is its inability to go beyond geographical accessibility (which is anyway outside the scope that the authors give themselves). This is important because accessibility is as much social as it is geographical. Proximity to health centres either by walk or by motorised mode may not actually result in accessing care when needed especially for some individuals or groups/communities depending on socio-demographic and other considerations. Despite having done a thorough job in terms of characterising physical/geographcial differences across the country, the inability to sufficiently engage with this underlying principle of access to healthcare is the only major overarching shortcoming in my assessment. I believe this can be addressed through a robust/comprehensive discussion to put their findings in perspective. I have outlined a few other issues that could be addressed to improve the utility of this paper to the readership. Since there are two line numbers, I use the one running from 1-478 (the other numbering scheme restarts on each page)

An important gap in my review is my inability to assess the statistical calculations underlying the mapping and Gini coefficient. I have some understanding of these and have gone through and found the reporting of this good, but I hope the journal will take input of another reviewer to ensure that the statistical and mapping approach used is good enough. 

1. On p.5, lines 97-100, the authors overstate the case for assessing geographcial accessibility (see above comment on a more comprehensive assessment of access to healthcare). This applies across the paper; see for eg. lines 131-134 on p.6 where again the case for identifying new public health facilities is being premised entirely upon an assessment of differences in geographical accessibility. Indeed, the overall equalisation of physical proximity to accessibility may require a thorough re-visit or at least a well articulated limitation acknowledging the non-equivalence.  
2. Compliments to the authors on well described section on data sources and methods in general which has been written in a way that allows replicability. 
3. Authors could describe the logic underlying the 7 ranges of duration that they have divided the time taken into. While initial ranges are of 15 mins, the last 4 are not so. How could this affect the analysis would deserve a comment/to be dealt with under limitations
4. The results related to the patterns of inequalities seen appear quite in line with expectations no? For eg. the relatively better-off accessibility in the central and southern terai vis-a-vis northern mountain? (Lines 222-225; p.9) or the differences in accessibility to secondary and tertiary vs primary (lines 228-230; p.9; see also p.14 lines 311-312). Overall the results in this section “geographical accessibility” are in line with what one would expect. 
5. p. 13, line 287 has a spelling mistake with “accessibility”
6. Some portions of the discussion bring up themes that were not specifically studied such as lines 313-316 on p.14. 
7. The discussion on telemedicine (lines 320-322; p.9) for eg. again assumes feasibility of various other health systems elements beyond “location”. There are both technological & social/cultural barriers to realising this and the authors might consider modulating the enthusiasm for this given such evidence. This again points to overemphasising on physical availability of care versus accessibility of care (and indeed acceptability of care)
8. The assertion regarding differences within provinces is very interesting pattern (lines 323-325; p.14). However, this also has too many confounders to be able to move forward as a possible pattern. Some of the within province socio-economic attributes might for eg explain this and in which case national level priority-setting for disadvantaged groups (top-down pressure) may compliment provindial/regional reforms. Consider a more nuanced discussion with respect to this pattern.
