The paper has great potential in contribution to literature on the contextual nature of COVID-19 pandemic in countries and particularly aims to share how the Indian response to scaling up testing happened. Paper identifies key events in relation to this scaling up of testing across the country and aims to share how this happened, possibly with the objective of informing practice elsewhere and perhaps critically reflecting and learning lessons from looking back at this scale-up effort (aims are unstated explicitly but inferred here by me based on the reading). The authors are a mix of implementers and researchers involved with the Indian governments’ nodal agency for testing preparedness, the Indian Council of Medical Research, a very welcome effort by practitioners to contribute to academic literature given their position at the policy “frontlines” of testing scale-up. 

However, the paper in its current form has a few important shortcomings in achieving these aims. I outline the overall main shortcomings to begin with and point out other relatively minor points too that need to be addressed. 
- At various points (as outlined in detailed comments below), the authors use adjectives to identify the importance of a particular step/decision/policy direction. However, they give scant details of this. For eg.  on p.4 (“Extreme caution was exercised to ensure high quality of results”, “robust sample disposal”), p.5 (“Arduous efforts were mounted to ensure availability of at least one rRT-PCR laboratory in every district”). The overall paper carries limited details or reflections on what logic were underlying these steps. What were the “strategies” so to speak that worked and what did not work? These kind of policy/implementation insights will greatly enhance the value of this paper to both global and in-country readership, but is currently lacking. 
- In line with the point above, the submission does not identify any specific lesson learned from this scale-up either to India, nor to global health (beyond broad gain identified in terms of need to sustain this expanded testing infrastructure). What were the specific lessons one learns from this looking back at a year of scaling up testing in India remains to be answered in my assessment. 
- The above two are illustrated by the fact that there is not a single reference/section of the submission that places the findings reported in context of what is already known or being reported from other similar contexts. For eg. what lessons for India’s neighbors in South Asia in terms of specific strategies? Or what lessons for other countries like India which may be struggling with scaling up testing? While a primary analysis of this may not be the primary purpose of the authors, at least locating their findings in the context of the global pandemic, other similar countries and/or within India’s neighbourhood is missing. 
- Overall, there is a lack of a critical reflection on “why things happened the way they did” or “how they happened”, but a focus on “what happened”. This diminishes the value of this important contribution. I believe there are very important lessons to learn from the submission, but they have not been integrated into the paper. For eg. the figures provide a wonderful summary of “what happened” with respect to the scale-up (for eg. figs 2, 3 & 4), but one expects that the accompanying text shall illuminate “how”these happened…what worked and what did not and how potential barriers were overcome etc. 


Detailed comments 1. Abstract lists “repurposing existing resources” and “strengthening existing infrastructure” among a list of other components of what authors call a “multi-pronged approach”. These two components have overlaps and have not been clearly detailed out as to what they constitute. Similarly, the overall nature of this multi-pronged approach remains unclear. Some reflection will help clarify: what made these “innovative”, what was the underlying strategy which facilitated/allowed these to happen…..
1. On p.3, COVID-19 has been characterised as the worst humanitarian crisis of “all time”. An optional suggestion for hte authors to reconsider this assertion. 
2. Characterisation of some nations as “developed” may be reviewed. Do the authors mean high-income countries?
3. On p.3, line 31: do authors mean “some laboratories” instead of “selected”? It changes the meaning if they say “selected”. Review
4. In p. 3, lines 42-50, authors talk about the sub-optimal laboratory infrastructure in India early on in the outbreak and then begin to describe how per day testing capacity was ramped up. Some analysis of what might have been considered optimal, what they aimed for in terms of population coverage, what kind of gaps existed for which they describe the strategies below are useful. For eg. were there regional gaps? Were gaps only in terms of buildings or HR as well? Which states/regions/populations were underserved (they use “underserved” in few locations but is unclear as to who these regions/populations are). 
5. Authors provide a bulleted list of 11 points that are their key lessons. How were these identified? Why these and not some others? Some description/analysis of why the authors identified these (and not others) and how they did this will be very helpful to global health readership. For eg. role of state governments and city administrations is not mentioned (perhaps they did not play much of a role? Or perhaps this is out of scope of this paper? There can be others like this and hence describing where the authors are coming from in terms of identifying these 11 will be useful to locate the perspective of this submission. 
6. Many instances were details are wanting. For eg. “robust sample disposal policies” are mentioned with no reference/link to what these were. Setting up of mentor institutes is mentioned as a measure that helped with little detail on what these mentor institutions did and how they specifically contributed to the scaling up beyond general details that they provided “inspection and validation”, “training” and “monitoring”.  For eg. was the choice of establishing these in institutions of eminence a good one in hindsight? Which ones did better and which ones not in terms of mentorship and outreach? What can we learn from such institutionalised mentorship mechanisms? 
7. On p. 5, in the section describing the establishment of rRT-PCR lab in every district of India, little more details will help understand the scale-up better. For eg. how many of these were entirely new and how many were repurposing of existing PCR infrastructure in medical colleges? Which districts were/are late to join and how did this impact the pandemic spread? How were districts prioritised - for eg was there a triaging of high-priority districts in terms of pandemic load or was the growth/scale-up organic based on motivation of the colleges? Many such important questions that are insightful to the readership are not elaborated. 
8. On p.5, section titled “Political commitment” could be reviewed in terms of fit of title to the content. Essentially, the content in this point is about government conducting evacuations and the medical council’s role in making medical colleges as testing centres. To me, it doesn’t sufficiently demonstrate “political commitment”. Suggest a narrower title for this section in line with what is presented. 
9. Yet another example of an important result that merits critical analysis/reflection (but missing) is onp.6 on the introduction of rapid antigen test. In lines 43-52, authors discuss about the diverse responses from state governemnts to the availability of RATs, but do not tell the reader why this was so. Also how did the uptake (or lack thereof) of RATs by the state affect their pandemic control and testing. What was done about this and what is the lesson we learn from this experience of ICMR remains unanswered. 
10. And finally, a conclusion that can tie together the several lessons embedded in the manuscript is lacking. Authors state that the “above strategies were largely successful” but do not tell us how this was assessed and what kind of reflections from implementation led to these. The one place where the authors resort to sharing “pitfalls” it is about the decisions made by state governments, whereas there is plenty of scope to reflect on the strategies analysed in this paper itself, which I feel is a missed opportunity. 
11. Both abstract and main text need substantial copy-editing. There are two consistent types of mistakes that make parts of the manuscript difficult to read: (a) lack of spacing between words where needed, excess spacing between words in several places, (b) Sentences starting without spacing after a period/full-stop.
