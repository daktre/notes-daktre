# Plos GPH: Indigenous Peoples and the COVID-19 pandemic: a systematic scoping review

Thank you for inviting me to review this paper. 

The review is a timely contribution to understanding the impact of COVID-19 on indigenous peoples. Various knowledge gaps based on the scoping review are reported in the Results. 

An overall comment on this paper is the need to locate the author's individual/institutional affiliations with respect to the idea of indigeneity. While of course there is a strong justification for anyone to undertake an exploration of the probable drivers of marginalization of indigenous communities, several research studies have shown that the direct involvement of indigenous peoples within such research enterprise addresses the epistemic gap in terms of understanding/framing the idea of indignity from a purely academic/action perspective. Such a participation has been lacking as the authors very likely acknowledge due to social & structural factors. So, in that sense perhaps some reflection on this aspect with respect to the current initiative which is the COVID observatories project or the author's Institute's would be useful (To some extent I noted the mentioning/acknowledging of several specific indigenous communities in the Acknowledgments, on which see later). 

The assertion in the introduction section on page five, line number 88 that indigenous people have been identified as being particularly vulnerable to COVID 19 is backed by an assertion by the Food and Agriculture Organization on COVID-19 and indigenous people. While this assertion is very likely to be true, there is a difficulty with respect to backing this assertion with good quality evidence, mainly because of the geographical and social margins in which many of the world's indigenous people live, which in turn, limits the quality and availability of data about the intensity of medical and social impact of COVID-19 on these communities. That said there is also good data to show differential exposures within specific indigenous population groups in a given country or region as well as differences in levels of exposure and impact across and within specific indigenous population groups. Some of these differences are of course, a function of relatively remote location whereas others are also due to differential degrees of marginalization within indigenous populations, for example, by gender or other identities and across indigenous population groups by their specific indigenous group identity. This kind of nuance while indeed reflected in the Discussion could be strengthened and wherever data/review remit does not allow this, then a robust limitation/reflection section may be needed. 

And wherever available, better evidence base to support the differential degree of impact within and across indigenous peoples may be needed. In my assessment, the lack of this is probably reflected in available literature and will have to be an important limitation of a scoping review that builds upon published scholarship. Yet another limitation, of course, is the fact that a lot of the probable impact on indigenous population is recorded or documented in journalistic and civil society voices and reports as opposed to peer reviewed publications. Authors do mention that the current analysis does not include such documents but how does this limit the results and the implications of their findings? 

On page six lines 103-105, the author's reflect on their choice of scoping review as a method as opposed to systematic review. I agree with this assertion. But they seem to indicate that while a systematic review has well defined questions, a scoping review may not have so I would encourage the authors to revisit these assertions and better frame the utility of a scoping review for their questions. For instance, I find that the authors have fairly clear and specific scoping review questions which they have framed. So, why would there be a need to mention that scoping reviews apply only for broader questions? In my assessment the flexibility offered by the scoping review method with respect to exploratory studies, as well as studies that seek to synthesize current knowledge base on aspects of social determinants or social inequalities etc. is a relative advantage for scoping reviews, rather than the specificity of research questions, which is perhaps not the best differentiator between scoping and systematic reviews. 

In table one on page eight, the author's indicate the inclusion of articles in all languages. The final language breakdown of the articles shortlisted however does not seem to show the language also there is the additional matter of how familiarity of the languages in which all the documents were identified within the research team has been addressed. 

On page nine the author's indicate what they term as initial analysis. Please provide a little more detail as to how this initial analysis was conducted. 

As is often the practice nowadays was a scoping review protocol registered or deposited anywhere? If yes a citation to that may be included. 

One of the results probably that will be useful is results related to absence of research or data. For instance, if there are top 10 or 20 countries or geographies where indigenous population have large number of proportions to what extent is the review results indicating research/data coming in from these geographies? I wonder if this is considered to be within the scope by the authors and if yes, since they probably do have the data in their current results to make assertions on this kind of geographical gaps they could consider doing so this is a suggestion which may be considered discretionary. This will also help us locate to the probable IPs whose scholarship has been included….and whose not? 

Given the fact that defining indigeneity as discussed by the authors on page six is contested sometimes even by national governments, and given that specific countries may be using non-English labels; and some countries may be using statutory identification labels which may not give the label “indigenous” to communities that would otherwise fit the United Nations definition of indigeneity; given this kind of legal and social and political context in multiple countries, how have the authors addressed this? For instance within South Asia, some of the terms used by indigenous people to self-identify may not at all have State sanction; and labels considered by the State may also sometimes not include “indegenous” and this would be reflected in reports or publications as well. 

To some extent, the section on page 15 on social and ecological challenges that indigenous peoples are experiencing confirms that a lot of pre-existing vulnerabilities and pre-existing social and structural inequalities have contributed to the social impact of COVID-19 on indigenous populations. I wonder whether the authors need to frame this section a bit better by distinguishing between the direct social and ecological effects of the pandemic versus social and ecological effects mediated through pre existing structural and social inequalities. Again, this is offered more as a suggestion given that these two interact with each other and often in specific micro contexts. The direct and indirect effects imperceptibly merge.  

On page 20 where the author's allude to the Indian Health Service and Indian reservations, perhaps given the global context of the paper, it may be useful to distinguish this from the South Asian Indian population as there has also been a bunch of global health assistance between countries and these should not be mistaken for such assistance. 

Interestingly, all the instances where the federal government provides financial assistance to specific indigenous peoples government appear to be in high income contexts such as USA, Canada and Australia, save for the instance of Brazil, where the court seems to have mediated such assistance. This review NOT finding such instances in lower and middle income countries too is significant in its absence. 

On page 22 in the discussion, the author's state that they provide a broad overview of the peer reviewed and grey literature, whereas I believe that the gray literature the review is exclusively documents related to the United Nations. Authors may consider clarifying this because there is a bunch of civil society generated grey literature in public domain, which understandably is very difficult to synthesize and has not been included. Their assertion in terms of coverage of grey literature will hence need to be moderated. 

The discussion does not yet sufficiently advance our understanding of social and ecological factors and COVID-19 among indigenous populations. I believe most of the social and ecological factors that the author's point out in this section were well known previously. And the expectation was how specifically these interacted with COVID-19. The results and discussion related to moving this interaction and its understanding further could be considered. Again, this is merely a discretionary suggestion in order to push the authors to elevate the utility of their review beyond what is already known. Potentially, this could also be a matter of framing the results that ought to be done better in discussion. 

The discussion on page 26 related to some pockets with relatively higher coverage of vaccination among indigenous peoples needs to be contextualized to the fact that the geographies that this is being reported from are all high income settings, where the coverage of non Indigenous communities was probably achieved earlier. Again, this calls for a more nuanced reporting of findings given the huge diversity in LMICs/global south. 

In the acknowledgments section the COVID observatories project acknowledges indigenous peoples and representatives from various geographies only by their indigenous label and no individuals have been named. In my experience with acknowledgments sections of journals while this is not very uncommon, the norm is in fact to name individuals who have made contributions to the current article but are not satisfying the requirements for authorship. Perhaps the author's could clarify why no individual from any of the several 10s of indigenous communities acknowledged have been named. Perhaps this is something to re-visit related to my earlier comment on locating the authors’ with respect to various indigenous population groups and the indigenous identity (see above).
