Thank you for the opportunity to review this submission. The authors use semi-structured interview based data gathered from patients attending a public hospital in urban setting in Kathmandu along with similar data from health workers and policymakers to create a framework that could help with designing interventions for patients with Diabetes. The submission is well written and shares insights on a relatively poorly understood topic within NCD care in LMIC settings. The paper highlights the prominent role played by cultural determinants in shaping healthcare behaviour in these settings. Submission requires further clarifications in the presentation of its results which I have outlined below. 

Major comments

1. Some short-forms (HBGLs, PEN) have not been expanded at the first instance that they occur. 
2. Some of the papers cited in the Introduction (refs number 18 and 19 for eg) are quite dated (from 2003), also from a journal whose abstract in unavailable (although Pubmed indexed). There must be more updated estimates/data on population prevalence of Diabetes in Nepal. Consider revising these. 
3. Context-specificity only for LMICs? One of the key themes that runs through the paper is the importance of context in shaping NCD care processes and outcomes. This is indeed widely known and highlighted and the authors rightly emphasise on this via their findings as well. However, there seems to be an assumption (even explicitly stated in line 106-107) that this context specificity is relevant only for LMIC settings. While I do see the evidence presented for high context-specificity in the setting they study, I dont see an argument for how this is not relevant in high-income settings too, or for that matter, any health system setting. I wonder if the authors rather want to emphasise that current NCD models perhaps are being imported from HIC contexts, which then makes their findings from Nepal (or other LMIC settings) more relevant. 
4. Cultural determinants: The overarching idea of “culture” and “cultural determinants” is seen throughout the paper. Constructs about what is “cultural” (and what is religious for eg) could be clarified in the background. As the authors note, there are quite some overlaps of this with gender as well as religion. In the lack of such conceptual clarity, the distinctions between some of the constructs appear difficult to tease apart. For example, there is the idea of “culturally compelling” intervention that the authors mention in line 110. They assert that - to them - this means that the intervention “…sit(s) within social and ecological environments”, further highlighting the need to clarify this at a conceptual level. Such an *a priori* stating of cultural determinants will also help readers understand some of hte results based on abstractions of data that the verbatim quotations illustrate.  Similar is the case with “cultural environment” that comes up in the realists (see line 251). Again, this too requires some amount of conceptualisation in order to understand what the authors considered as the cultural environment in the given setting. 
5. Methods and tools confusion: Among the “range of methods” mentioned by the authors are some tools as well (e.g.food frequency questionnaires mentioned as methods). 
6. Income as a proxy for socio-economic position: Authors use income as a proxy for socio-economic position. Their results include assertions drawn from applying such proxies. However, in several south Asian settings, social positions are quite complex; while income levels are only one among several markers of social/socio-economic positions in society. Other non-income markers including particular caste/cultural groupings also influence positions. While this may not be remedied by re-analysis, it should certainly be kept in mind while writing the results or stated as a limitation in my assessment. Other problems also exist with respect to using income as a proxy for social/SE position. In the results, authors seem to use this as a proxy for “status” (vs position). 
7. Inferring influences: Some of the results have to do with x influencing y (eg. lines 256-7; lines 296-97; line 376). I am not sure that the authors present enough data to show this, more so in their specific context. I agree with their assertion broadly speaking in many such instances. But in a paper that sets out a case for context-specific evidence, there is inadequate evidence presented to make assertions of influences in one direction. In the case of one such assertion on socio-economic status (sic!) influencing behaviour, the quotation included to illustrate this is that of a health worker perception of such an association. The authors need to present more compelling evidence from particular themes that show processes linking particular health behaviours manifesting as a result of being in a particular socio-economic context. A further example is the “strong influence” that they report on patients from social relationships. There is no data presented on how the “strong” was inferred. 
8. Data from the structured component of the semi-structured tool: No data has been presented from the survey tools or from the structured data collected, although references to such data have been made in the results. For eg. in lines 265-267, authors mention that their data indicates that the urban poor have been shown to have “lack of health knowledge…”. I do not see such data having been presented. It would be useful to see some of the descriptive data in tables to support these assertions. Similarly, assertions of “…our data found…” come up again in line 271. 
9. Unhealthy diet: Unhealthy diet is mentioned many times but not well defined. In some instances, dietary behaviour that could be construed as “cultural” is assumed to be “unhealthy”. I believe this could be because for patients with Diabetes, “sweets” (which are a part of the local culture) are prohibited/restricted, albeit not necessarily “unhealthy”. I would suggest a careful revision of instances where this term is used. If possible, an initial conceptualising of what constitutes “unhealthy” (for eg, is this being used for patients with Diabetes, or in general…) may be needed. Similarly, lack of “health knowledge” seems to have the same conceptual problem. Are authors referring to awareness of dietary restrictions for Diabetes patients (as per modern medicine) or a general lack of awareness of what constitutes healthy behaviour. In the case of latter, there are huge overlaps between the positionally of the authors and that of local culture and hence this need for clarity. (Eg  “urban poor’s lack of health knowledge” on Iine 266, patients did not know of healthy eating - line 278)
10. Cultural determination vs individual agency: At some instances, there is a tension between explanations at the level of individual agency vs those at the level of cultural determinants. Authors seem to have settled for the latter without sufficient clarity on how this was done. See for eg. instances where patients chose to indulge in sweets despite being aware of their effects. Even if this cannot be settled one way or another in the current analysis, this needs to be explained either in results or as a limitation. 
11. The physical environment and gender sections are very well described. Compliments on these to the authors. On gender, see note about culture above (authors too acknowledge the overlap). 
12. Political and economic environment has only been described from the “elite” view of policymakers and researchers. This is a limitation of the study. Patient perspectives on this has not been presented. This will have to be acknowledged and accounted for either as a limitation or under discussion. What could be the effects of this on their analysis?
13. In the political and economic environment results, the authors embrace “success” of government campaigns, policies and legislative initiatives on one hand (line 324) while later on mentioning that they have not been evaluated (line 334). In general this section needs strengthening with respect to how the authors make the assertions. I feel this section has much more “discussion” than results, but I may be wrong. See for example assertions about decentralisation that has occurred recently in Nepal mentioned din line 361. Was this explored in their interviews? Overall, my suggestion is for this section - if possible - to undergo a rewriting based on re-analysis and/or more quotations from data. 
14. Lines 480: Assertions about whether Daughters-in-laws (DIL) were sufficiently educated (or not) are not illustrated with any data. Moreover, alternate explanations are not considered. For instance, several qualitative studies show the power dynamics in such households could push DILs to win the favour of MILs through “tasty” (often using more oil/sugar) food. Have the authors considered such alternative explanations for these?
15. In figure 1, culturally appropriate food is situated separate from cultural practices. Please consider this comment along with my earlier comments on conceptual clarity on “culture” , “cultural practices”, “cultural environment” and “cultural determinants”. 
16. In many LMIC settings especially in large cities, the formal and informal private sector including unqualified practitioners play an important role in care provision and influence household health behaviour. Their influence is possibly significant if Kathmandu is anything like other south Asian urban settings in India/Bangladesh. The people interviewed in this study do not represent viewpoints from any of these actors. Consider clarifying this in the limitations. 

Minor/discretionary comments

1. Line 60: Sentence starting with “When we consider dietary behaviour…” appears redundant
2. Line 112: “We hope that our model can work as a guide…” Consider revising. While the optimism is appreciated, it could be a better idea to list/outline possible applications of the findings. 
3. Line 371: Sugar is a wider south Asian term for Diabetes. Something for the consideration of the authors. 
4. Line 402: Authors probably mean “spike” instead of “spoke” 
5. Line 497: “The literature…” qualify.
