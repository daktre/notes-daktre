Thank you for the opportunity to review a revised version of the original paper. I have noted the authors response to earlier reviewers (I was not among them). The authors provide justification to justify retention of points that were brought up by one of the reviewers.   Overall, I find the article to be novel in extending the idea of perceived coercion that comes from mental health literature onto a the issue of lockdowns such as the one we recently saw in response to COVID-19. However, there are still two major shortcomings. Firstly, the final set of papers that the authors review are relatively small, regionally diverse yet un-representative of major populations globally, and thematically address disparate/disjoint and often non-overlapping issues related to the lockdown effects. This makes the task of tight summarising/synthesising of the results challenging as well as carefully specifying the boundries/limits of these assertions (as limitations) a pre-requisite. I believe these two points need to be substantively addressed by (a) carefully and rigorously “nuancing” the assertions made in the Results to bound them/frame them as being applied to specific contexts/regions/populations/sub-populations, and (b) in substantively discussing the lack of globalising these findings as a limitation. And secondly, see post no. 5 on the methodological issue of “gaps in knowledge”. 

This - for me - remains the only major shortcoming that needs to be addressed before moving forward. I outline below a few specific comments, some of which are part of the comment above but a few other minor points as well. 

1. **Abstract:** The last 2-3 lines which summarise the key results of the study appear to be overly abstract/satellite view of the studies. While these are acceptable as results, I suggest the authors to re-visit the framing of what the key results of this review are. Currently, both points related to “absence of standardiosed measures” and “better understanding of cultural and socioeconomic factors” are both well known. I believe the authors do have specific review findings that they could report in the abstract. 
2. The track-changes document contains unresolved commentary between co-authors that should ideally have been resolved before submission. 
3. On the issue highlighted above about limited regional/sub-regional/ethnic/indigenous/socio-demographic patterning of results at smaller scales, see for eg. sections in the Results (esp. theme 1) where assertions are made at global level. These require to be more modestly framed and attributed to specific populations/regions/demographies based on the study they are coming from. For instance, (a) on p.9 “most participants expressed willingness to…” (begging the question where? and which kind of participants?), (b) on p.23 “…correlated with belief that political leaders are competent….(where was this study because perceptions of trust on elected representatives or political institutions is widely variable even within countries), (c) on p.23 again “…some studies suggested that participants from economically developing countries” (aside from the fact that the category economically developing countries is problematic, it is unclear which kind of participants/people held this perception; where they too from HIC/LIC/LMIC), (d) “individuals with right wing political leanings…” (given that what is considered “right wing” is differently perceived and understood both in global health and within/across countries, this and other findings like this will need a bit more contextualisation), (e) on p.24, “similarly low-income participants were less in favour of lockdown” (again, where?); also see later on same page reporting findings which need regionalisation/contextualisation, (f) on p.28, see “Those opposed to lockdown tended to express greater distrust in authorities” (again this is not a globalisable finding no? so where was this or else in what kind of settings was this this the case?)
4. On p.4, fix the sentence synax/grammar: “The purpose of this review is…”  
5. The authors (on p.5) assert their intention to share “gaps in knowledge” and later on have an entire section on p.32 on “Areas for future research”. I find this challenging to review. Methodologically speaking, how were these gaps identified? Was an *a priori* framework used to identify this? For instance, based on my reading of the manuscript, I can identify various other gaps and this depends on the position/framework of the reader. In the lack of that, it is tough to critically assess if the gap identification was based on the evidence reviewed (or not). I would suggest to the authors to moderate/drop this objective as well as the section on p.32 on areas for future research as these appear to lack substantial justification in the methods. Even without these, the article still has important contributions. 
6. There have been extensive commentary on lockdowns and their perceived effects in grey literature and in-depth analysis of various narratives on these. Some of these narratives go far beyond descriptive journalistic pursuits to more deeply inquire into the diversity of lockdown impacts faced by several vulnerable groups. The review’s lack of engagement with this must be acknowledged as a limitation. 
7. On p.6, the authors have now added a few lines (in track-changes) starting with “Examples of excluded search terms were….”. This is a bit puzzling because many of these were part of the search strategy. Please review this portion carefully once more. 
8. On. p.25, use of the word “chronicled” may be reviewed. One of the papers attributed to be such a “chronicle” is a qualitative study of predictors. 
9. Review the bringing up of a systematic review as a citation (on p.24). IS this the only one on this topic?
10. “Conspiracy theory” is brought up based on its study from 1 of the papers authors reviewed. This comes back again in the Discussion where there is reference to Whatsapp messaging and specific political leaders (Trump for example). I am not too convinced on the way in which this topic has been handled as there is very little empirical basis presented by the authors to be able to make these assertions in the results and discuss. Please review this critically. 
11. One of the issues is that phenomena such as “collective social norms” (which appears on p.26 in the context of a study from Indonesia and Malaysia) is one of several phenomena of interest that the authors bring up in their review which is highly contextual, socio-cultural and political and requires in-depth and contextualised inquiry. However, this has not been substantively discussed and is often presented in a globalised way. For eg. the collectivistic social norms that were important enough to emerge from the study they reviewed from Indonesia and Malaysia may not at all be powerful enough to explain similar outcomes, say in Morocco or in UK. How do we then moderate our assertions to be able to convey such a nuance? (see point 3)
12. Some of the topics that come up in discussion were outside the remit of the review. Please carefully review the Discussion to remove these. For instance, see the section on p.31 on “what level of restriction is justified for what level of risk of harm….”
