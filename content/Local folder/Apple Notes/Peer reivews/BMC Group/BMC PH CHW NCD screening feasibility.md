Thank you for the opportunity to review this paper. The paper describes an evaluation of an intervention aimed at assessing feasibility of improving early detection, treatment and prevention of a set of NCDs in a rural setting in one of the north Indian states, by training a group of people matching the age and educational qualifications of the community health workers of the state government health services. The background, methods and results section are concise and well written. Their use of HPV testing of self-sampled vaginal specimens collected at homes via health workers is indeed novel and of relevance in several limited resource settings for implementing population screening and strengthening primary health care services. 

**Major comments**
1. CHWs or CHW-like health workers: The central question that the paper promises to answer (going by the reading of the title and by the background) is whether CHWs (according to the authors’ description, the health workers within the primary health care delivery system that is financed and managed by the state governments in Indian states) can provide home-based screening for common NCDs. However, what the study actually demonstrates is that the same could be effectively delivered by people of similar educational background like CHWs. I believe their pilot results indicate that health workers can be trained to provide home-based screening for NCDs. Beyond this, to move to the assertion that current CHWs within the primary care apparatus of the state can provide the intervention designed by them to the same level of effectiveness as compared to their own group of health workers (recruited outside of the CHW cadre, not employed/associated with the current government health services) was not assessed by their intervention (on this more in point number 2 below). Per se, this is not a problem in the paper itself, but rather in the framing of the title, results and the discussion which assume the former. I would propose to amend the title, results and discussion to reflect this and/or add a section on limitations highlighting this. 
2. Generalisability to all rural settings in India: Healthcare is a state subject in India. Hence, Indian states have organised their health services differently albeit all of them structured around the primary health care based design. Indian states also have different levels and diverse varieties of private sector providers who are providing a lot of health services. Rural settings in India are also very diverse with huge variations in terms of caste, socio-economic stratification and gender dynamics both within and across states. Given this larger national context, it is not appropriate to generalise the study findings to rural settings in India. While I agree that other similar settings such as the one described by the authors may also achieve similar results, this needs to be more sharply asserted rather than making assertions for rural Indian populations with respect to their study. (see for eg. the conclusion in the abstract as well as sections in the results and discussion)
3. Improve description of the study setting: Very closely related to the point above is the lack of sufficient descriptions of the context. The section on study design and context is too short for the readers to make an assessment of the kind of settings in which the authors’ intervention are likely to yield benefit. I would suggest to include sufficient information on the block/taluka/sub-unit that the intervention was implemented in such as basic socio-demographic profile including population, overall socio-economic and healthcare infrastructure status including public and/or private sector providers etc. This will allow readers to also compare the study population with the wider population in the block. Also, some of the descriptions could be amended to clarify (a) whether the block has more than the 20 villages that were selected in the study, (b) whether the block has more PHCs than the one PHC included in the study, and other information on the local health services in the block that will help the reader understand the study setting better. For eg. how does this block compare to the other blocks in the district in terms of the government health services performance (is it among the better off or worse-off), or for instance how well staffed are the PHCs and other healthcare infrastructure in the block. 
4. Clarify current guidance on NCD services availability: The authors assert in their background (on line 7-11) that the nearest community clinics, which they consider as equivalent to sub-centres are the point of care for NCD care. I believe this may be a mis-reading of the current guidance which I paste below (Source: National Program for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, CVD and Stroke( NPCDCS) guidelines accessible from the National Health Portal of India). On p.7 of this guidance which I reproduce below (the version I refer to is available here: http://health.puducherry.gov.in/ACTS%20AND%20MANUALS/Operational_Guideline_NPCDCS.pdf), the document identifies CHCs/district hospitals where NCD clinics are supposed to be established. While I agree with the authors position (as echoed by international guidance including WHO’s Global NCD action plan that care has to be decentralised nearest to homes, at least to PHCs), this is not part of the current guidance. 
“NC Clinic will be established at CHC and District Hospital (NCD here refers to Cancer Diabetes, Hypertension, Cardiovascular diseases and Stroke) where comprehensive examination of patients referred by lower health facility /Health Worker as well as of those reporting directly will be conducted for ruling out complications or advanced stages of common NCDs. Screening, diagnosis and management (including diet counseling, Lifestyle management) and home based care will be the key functions.”

So there are two things to be addressed: (1) The fact that the current guidance does not in fact identify a role for screening either at sub-centres/PHCs but rather for CHCs/District hospitals, both of which are above PHCs in the organisation of health services in India, (2) the authors’ assertions in the background to this effect. 

1. Preventive healthcare in higher centres: The authors’ seem to imply (in lines 14-17 of their background) that higher centres may be providing “preventive care”. This needs to be amended in my opinion. Higher centres (I assume authors mean either taluka/block/district hospitals or specialty/medical college hospitals) typically focus much more (proportionally) on curative services than on preventive care. Bulk of preventive health services are offered by ASHAs (village level) sub-centres (cluster of villages) and PHCs (block/sub-block). 
2. Results (including the tables) are well written. 
3. The purported equalisation/“similarisation” of CHWs who are within the system with the health workers socially recruited for the study is problematic due to the following. These need to be either addressed as a limitation or in the discussion. (a) CHWs are in charge of implementing a variety of disease control programs that are often operating in silos and are often lacking in coordination with each other with respect to the time of the CHW needed for fulfilling responsibilities in their respective programs. Programs also often have parallel reporting requirements thus completing with CHWs time. If the authors intervention were to be implemented by the health services CHWs, then their intervention would have to compete with various other responsibilities of the CHW especially including reproductive and child health services which take a bulk of their time/resources and this could have had an impact on their results, (b) CHWs, unlike in the study implemented, do not work in pairs. Indeed, what the authors have done in their study may be better off for the outcomes, but this is not the case within the health services, (c) supervision of CHWs differs within the health services, as opposed to the study intervention where the study’s single aim was to address NCDs, and hence their supervision strategy might also have emphasised much more on this, (d) motivation and work culture within the local health services also has a bearing on CHW performance, which may not be reflected in the study. This is especially relevant to the framing of the first paragraph of the Discussion regarding “validation” of the model. See also the framing of the conclusion, especially the first line. 
4. Exclusion of pregnant women needs to be justified especially because the authors aim was to address common NCDs. Among the relatively neglected health problems in pregnancy is gestational diabetes. 
5. In conclusion, the authors call for a larger study. However, in my assessment, it appears that an implementation research design in partnership with the local health services is needed to still assess this strategy directly implemented by CHWs working within the system before undertaking a larger study. To my reading, the study results in fact demonstrate a proof of concept of sorts for taking this strategy into the local health services, rather than a scaling up directly. I would suggest to frame the discussion and conclusion in this well defined manner rather than a larger study, but I would leave it to the authors to appropriately frame their discussion while integrating these observations in the review. 

Minor/optional comments 

1. Please carefully revise the use of “the” and “a/an” in the paper. At several instances (especially in the abstract; see for eg. 2 instances of “the” in the fist line of the background in the abstract, and later in the last line of the abstract in the conclusion), definite article “the” has been used instead of an indefinite article “a”/“an” in my opinion. 
2. In line 11 of abstract, Indian is used instead of “India”
3. In the first paragraph of the results section authors asset “None of the participants ever had screening for NCDs”. Consider reframing as “None of the participants reported ever being screened for NCDs”
