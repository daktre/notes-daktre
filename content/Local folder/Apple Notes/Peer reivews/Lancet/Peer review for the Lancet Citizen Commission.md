# CoI

VP is chairing an advisory board for a grant on which I am lead PI.  SD and AM are collaborators on an ongoing grant that is unrelated to the funders of this study or the Lancet
KMS is a donor to a project in the organisation in which I have no role, but I am the Director of the organisation and hence there is an indirect competing interest. 

INTERNAL (NOT SENT) - I have made public remarks against policy positions taken by DS earlier on technical and public health evidence-base grounds. 

Reviewer comments to author

Thank you for the opportunity to read and review this important article by the *Lancet Citizens’ Commission on Reimagining India’s Health System*. The premise of this Lancet Commission and the output being reviewed are bound to shape the future course of health policy and programs discourse in various health policy and systems research, public health and civil society/social movement communities that have interest in this.   Given the ambitious nature of the enterprise that the commission has courageously taken on, and given the spillover of its mandate far beyond the hard evidence that there is likely (or not) to be on these topics, I would like to identify a few key influences and perspectives that underly this review. I hope this will help the authors to better understand why I say what I say, especially in instances where I do not cite the best/good quality evidence in favour of my assertions. While I do so, I strongly feel, given the nature of the enterprise, the authors too go through a process of critically inquiring and reflecting upon which of their own social positions and influences are likely to have shaped their contributions into the report. I say this at the outset primarily because the commission has chosen to position themselves as a *Citizen’s* commission and demonstrating this reflexivity in instances where experts taken on the mandate of speaking for the citizen ought to ideally reflect on this. This will greatly help situate the report within the wider citizen deliberations and dialogues that are bound to follow. This will also help better justify their own arguments wherever some of their arguments (as I point out below) are not rooted in any specific strong/best/quality evidence-base. 

I make overall comments on the report at the outset. Subsequently, I have made more specific comments often referring to specific pages or sections of the report.

1. Commission asserts itself as a citizens commission and provides details as to how citizens views have been incorporated. I see  two ways in which citizen engagement has been achieved. The first is the conduct of service, which were conducted by third-party agencies, and the second seems to be by way of consultations where a number of people seem to have been invited. While the former is a very limited way of engagement where a lot of control was within survey instruments and enquiries instituted by researchers, the lack probably includes largely "experts". It is also unclear what kind of people and what kind of representativeness of various citizen, social, economic political and cultural identities were kept in mind in the invited space that the commission created for these engagements. I find rather weak basis for accepting the assertion of this being a citizen commission. Act, the very least, the report will have to critically reflect upon how it has understood and conceptualised citizen, as well as how It has understood and conceptualised citizen engagement. For example, on the latter, the choice of methods do not demonstrate any effort at engaging with community based organisations, social movements, or with representatives of oppressed, caste, and tribes or marginalised people, who are in fact, several places in the report identified as important actors to engage with.
2. There is a lack of coherence between some recommendations and policy options. The commission seems to have been aware of these contestations and seems to have dealt with it by taking the position that they provide several policy options which Intern will be subject to wider dialogue process among policy makers or other stakeholders post publication. This is problematic because it requires the reader to equally give weightage to options that are sometimes not reconcilable with another C. For example, the options discussed under managed care vis-a-vis the related to public provision and comprehensive primary health care.


Specific comments

**On keywords & overall framing of citizen engagement:** The keywords do not include social determinants of health nor equity. This is puzzling given that the report needs to be a report of a commission imagining India's health system for the citizen they only keyword representing the citizen is the keyword citizen engagement, which in itself is not well conceptualised in my assessment (see my comments elsewhere). The team needs to not only just include new keywords, but might need to have a rethink on the framing of the report returns of how social determination of health and equity have been represented.

**On representation of CPHC in abstract:** In the abstract, there is a framing issue with the sentence on comprehensive primary healthcare. "Comprehensive primary healthcare has fallen short of meeting peoples needs". I believe the report aims to problematize the implementation, the conceptualisation or the way in which comprehensive primary healthcare has been rolled out rather than challenging the concept of comprehensive primary healthcare itself. At least this is the sense that emerges from a reading of the full report. However, this line in the abstract appears to miscommunicate the position of the report with vis-a-vis comprehensive primary healthcare. Please re-examine this framing/reporting in the abstract/executive summary. 

**On the justification for re-imagination**: The justification for re-imagining India's health system is well presented both the executive summary as well as in the report itself. The section related to how this is a historically unique moment in the executive summary is also very well formulated and conceptualised. Indeed, this section excellently and explicitly acknowledge rights and health equity as a core value of UHC and the reduction of inequities as a measure of progress across UHC goals, ….a sense that has not been adequately captured in the full content or the keywords as indicated above. The executive summary also rightly brings the focus on the problematic system versus issues at individual level, something that the Health Policy and systems research literature has consistently been highlighting.

**On conceptualising health system thinking**: Page 12 in the section on economic liberalisation and the advent of health system thinking, the way in which Health Systems thinking has been understood and used is limited to the WHO Health Systems building blocks. However, the literature on conceptualising and practising system thinking in health has evolved  beyond the building blocks framework of the WHO. This section needs to be improved to be better in line with the development in the health systems thinking field both within India and in global health.

**Check citation appropriateness:** Multiple instances in the report, the citations included need to be carefully examined to see if the assertion for which they are cited is fully supported by the citation. C. For example, reference number 52, which is cited at multiple location across the report in support of rapid deployment of diagnostic capacities and AI based screening. Reference, however, does not at all sufficiently carry evidence for this. This citation has been used for many other related assertions like this.

**On conceptualisation of health systems governance and some missing citizen issues:** (a) On page 21, health systems governance has been conceptualised largely as administration of the health department of the government and as data systems. Even conceptualising regulations appears to be missing from this way of conceptualising health systems governance. There are also some missing actors/institutions related to role of panchayats and local governments and urban local bodies and their increasing role that is required and is missing in urban areas. (b) The citizens/citizen oriented issues such as medical negligence, grievance, redressal, and denial of care, all of which have been subject to various kinds of research and documentation, both in review literature and in print/electronic media. The do not find adequate mention and have not been adequately considered to be a problem despite these being important/primary citizen-oriented issues with doctors, healthcare establishemnts and health policy/systems. (c) See, for example, several pieces of literature on adaptive governance post COVID-19: “An adaptive governance framework that relies on shared accountability while reconciling varying levels of flexibility across three dimensions—operational processes, institutional linkages and health system values, is the way to build a resilient health system in India.”, (d) See for example reform action 5 on transparent and accountable governance does not seem to have engaged with the persistent issue of informal payments and corruption, both within the health services for procurement and such as well as payments sort by providers for apparently free services at the point of service delivery. There is some empirical work done on this within India. Furthermore, emerging literature on adaptive governance shows the importance of having a strategic/vision level centralisation while allowing fore more operational/plannign/adaptation focused decentralisation. There is quite some literature on how to organise and manage multi-level governance approaches, on more adaptive governance approaches etc which does not seem to have been covered. Overall the governance reform action is also relatively weakly conceptualised, and inadequately linked to the ToC, the ToC principles and recommendations. 

**On malnutrition and rising inequities:** On p.25, the NFHS5 has been mentioned, but the alarming rise of severe malnutrition in multiple Indian states more so among intersectional sub groups has not been sufficiently dealt with. See for eg. Ulahannan SK, Wilson A, Chhetri D, Soman B, Prashanth NS. Alarming level of severe acute malnutrition in Indian districts. BMJ Glob Health. 2022 Apr;7(4):e007798. doi: 10.1136/bmjgh-2021-007798. PMID: 35450860; PMCID: PMC9024259 & Kochupurackal, S.U., Channa Basappa, Y., Vazhamplackal, S.J. *et al.* An intersectional analysis of the composite index of anthropometric failures in India. *Int J Equity Health* **20**, 155 (2021). https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-021-01499-y. The 2 papers indicated here are not meant to or suggested for inclusion but to illustrate the need for a citizen’s commission to engage with issues/areas which appear to be on the rise such as malnutrition, anaemia etc. 

**On the inferences from nationally representative(?) surveys:**  On page 30, various results of the assessment of availability of an access to services have been shared. These seem to come from a survey designed for national representation. However, while this may indeed be the case (on this more information/insight would be useful), they do not convey on ground situation of several communities (because of their aim for national representation). See for example, the assertion that 82% of households from marginalised tribes sought care… While this may be true for the survey population aggregated at a national level, there are several districts of the country and specific communities of Adivasis that do not see this kind of healthcare seeking desire/utilisation. Suggest to carefully reframe these assertions that are emerging from a survey with a very specific design implemented at the national level so as not to over-represent the findings. 

**On the research/knowledge eco-system for citizen’s voice:** Overall, there does not seem to be a focus on the health policy/systems reserch/or in general research/knowledge eco-system and its role (except for the learning health systems mention - for which see my comments). 

**On coherence between ToC, principles from the ToC, recommendations & reform actions:** There needs to be a better link and a coherence across ToC, the principles arising from the ToC and the reform actions. There are few reform actions which do not seem to be strongly linked to the ToC. How did the ToC and what the report calls “principles arising from the ToC” result in identifying the reform options and recommendations thereafter. This link is important to understand the role of the ToC in shaping the recommendations and reform options. If the ToC has been the central outcome of the community/citizen engagement consultations, then its primacy in driving reform choices and recommendations is high and this link needs to be better established or navigated for the reader. 

**Evidence-base for reform action 3: (a)** Evidence base presented in favour of refund action three is rather thin. The report does not adequately engage with several evidence in literature, which point towards the limited capacity of state governments in India in being able to effectively steer/manage/oversee contracting arrangements with the private sector for strategic purchasing. See for example, some of the citations of research cited, which explain the regulatory failure in Indian states with respect to health. Given this regulatory capacity in health in many states, some of them  in Southern India, which is supposed to have relatively better regulatory capacities, reform action 3 stands on thin ice. See for example, the phrase" accompanied by necessary regulations and mechanisms to ensure patient rights, accountability, provider, payment, reforms, and try setting, voluntary health insurance through commercial companies for cooperative organisations can be used to pool and pre-pay for private sector services…" - there are no such evidence which demonstrate these conditions to have been realised in any state in India to my knowledge, nor are there even single case studies on such accomplishments. (b) Further, under policy action 3, the idea of non-profit and social enterprise working for vulnerable populations and in underserved areas versus for-profit arrangements elsewhere where healthcare markets thrive is a problematic one. Although the report itself does not directly make an assertion in the previous sentence as I do, there is an underlying implication that the not-for-profit sector should continue in those areas where healthcare markets do not wish to venture, for obvious profit reasons. This implication is directly in contest with rights-based approaches to healthcare. However, the report does 

**On the managed care reform option, its justification and contestations with others:** On p.69, the paragraph starting with “One significant innovation in private sector health systems…..” Is problematic for reasons outlined earlier as well, but some specifics may be helpful to illustrate some general observations made above using this as an example. The report cites good evidence that shows the pitfalls of managed care in settings where governance/regulatory capacity is poor. The report cites specific studies too that have researched the regulatory failures and governance gaps in multiple Indian states vis-a-vis contracting private sector care. Several papers are available which outline the difficulties that state governance have in (1) drafting and negotiating contracts, (2) monitoring & evaluation of care provided by PPPs/private sectors, (3) defining quality and care standards and assessing if the quality is in line with these standards, (4) safeguarding public safety, interests and redressing grievances, denial of care and rights violations if they occur. Given this policy analysis evidence available, the report in its current form does not offer strong enough justification for inclusion of managed care. Citation of country experiences in Latin American settings is not sufficient in my assessment for this. This is of particular importance because the Commission and the report assert that they are keeping “citizens” at the center, and at several locations mention “lived experience” of citizens to be foregrounded. Given this, the Commission must either (1) diminish/remove the “citizen” foregrounding, (2) better justify/mitigate the amplification to managed care, (3) at the very leat have a much stronger outlining of the thinness of evidence in its favour. 

**On primary health care versus primary care in private sector:** On p.70, there is an important conceptual issue with section 3.3 with what the report identifies as “private sector primary healthcare services”. None of the content in this section appears to identify “primary healthcare” services but are rather various/specific curative/preventive services. Characterising these as private sector PHC services could be misleading in my assessment. Perhaps the report means “primary care” services - services which are offered at the primary level of the 3 tiered health services? This needs conceptualised better and connected to the wider policy reform action 3. 

**On communicating reimagined PHC care settings:** On p.72, the illustration of a reimagined PHC care seeing is shown and is very creatively done and has great potential for wider citizen engagement. It is indeed an example of an output of the commission that is citizen-focused but it is unclear if this is a sample and how it is located within teh wider plan of the commission. I assume this is an example of a type of communication tool that will be used? With whom, how? Does the commission plan to make this more widely available in various languages and in what format? Is this one of a series of illustrations in which are other PHC components not covered in this are covered in others in the series? Conceptualising and locating this panel within this wider plan may be useful. 

**On well-intentioned innovations like SDL/digital tech versus what is known about their equity & effectiveness:** Section on SDL built by public of Private sectors, once again, the evidence base cited is  thin. The sections are pointing towards well intended aspirations from digital technologies as opposed to where tested solutions that has worked for common citizens, the primary stakeholder whose interests the report seeks to foreground.

**On the coneptualisation and integration of learning health systems into the report:** Reform action 6 on fostering a learning health system is by far the weakest in terms of conceptualising what we know about characteristics of and how a learning system operates. Learning Health Systems is an important and possibly complex reform option that carefully addresses the internal/institutional reforms at local health systems level, at district level, and at state levels in terms of the kind of leadership, work, culture, and such system software elements which are needed in order to advance equity and innovation. The inclusion of learning health systems as a reform action is hugely appreciated. However, the literature on LHS has evolved over the last decade to identify deeper levels of learning that result in building new kinds of norms and institutionalising a new way of working that transform healthcare organisations, often referred to as triple loop learning. The WHO Alliance for Health Policy and systems research as well as various resources, including journal articles on this outline, the complex pathway through which such kind of deeper and institutional learning could occur. However, none of these find sufficient integration into the report. See for example Abimbola & Sheikh flagship report of the WHO Alliance for Health Policy & Systems Research on triple-loop learning and how this ties into the conceptualisation of LHS and its integration in the report (https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/344891/9789240032217-eng.pdf?sequence=1)

Minor/optional comments
Across the report, there is an inconsistent way in which some words have been capitalised mid-sentence please review. For instance, state has inconsistently carried a capital S in many instances of its occurrence even when it is supposed to stand for state government as opposed to the *State*.

On page 14, the short paragraph preceding section 3 needs to be revisited for its accuracy in summarising section 2. There are some very broad assertions made in this paragraph which have not been illustrated in the section.

Please reconsider the use of the term *allopathy* in the report which inconsistently appears to indicate modern medicine. See for instance, on page 16.

On page 20, the assertion about all public sector facilities, which are indeed automatically enrolled and and receive payments for services needs to be carefully checked with what is happening on ground, especially at the level of primary health Centre.

On page 21, the role of the Central Government has been mentioned as only being limited to overseas national level policy making and planning, while in fact as illustrated in their own previous section, the report does talk about the fragmented way in which multiple Central government ministries also run hospitals and healthcare establishments of their own. See for example, the armed forces and the labour department.

On page 29 an Initiative that was begun in 2013 has been mentioned. However, despite over a decade of its existence, the potential output/outcome of this initiative has been mentioned in future tense. Given over a decade existence. This initiative, the report could go into greater detail as to what the learnings are for the citizens commission from this initiative.

On p.51, the 2nd paragraph has several initiatives which do not really carry strong empirical basis and seem to possibly have entered through championing of their cause at consultaiotns/events. Suggest the team to carefully think through which of these they would like to include, endorse and what woudl the underlying justification be for doing so. 

On p. 54-55, there is a mention of “community engagement mechanisms found most effective….” - These are unclear and nothing specific is cited and it is unclear who examined, and what outcomes were the ones for which they were effective.
