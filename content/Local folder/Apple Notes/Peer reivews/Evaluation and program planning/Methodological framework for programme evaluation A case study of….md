Thank you for the opportunity to review this paper. The article asserts two aims: (a) “highlight and describe the methodologies used to conduct a programme evaluation, (b) illustrate the “…concurrent nested design with mixed method approach” based on the authors’ description of this approach being used in an evaluation of a community based mental health programme in a particular setting in Kolkata, India. 

I believe there is are major issues in the paper:
- One of the aims not having been clearly dealt with
- Methodology/method confusion?
- Background is limited to mixed methods whereas the title, abstract and aims situate the paper within larger body of program evaluation
- A clearly 

Major comments

- The abstract does not provide a summary of the authors’conslusions/results. After stating two aims and mentioning that the paper reports a case study, the single line result/conclusion mentions the need for “reflective work that considers methodologies in context”. The final line “The experience will…” again is too generic. Request the authors to clearly identify the main lessons from their case study and include them into abstract, keeping in mind that the readers of the paper would be interested in “new” knowledge and/or insights from their case study. 
- The aims need to be clearly stated. It appears that the authors’ first aim (see above) is quite broad and encompasses a wide swathe of program evaluation literature and practice, whereas the second one is quite specific. The relationship between the two are also unclear. 
- The methods and results do not address their first aim. When the authors aim to describe the methodologies used to conduct programme evaluation, it is expected that they will prepare a synthesis of various methodologies (not only methods/tools) described in literature. However, in the Methods section, the authors begin with the description of the context of a particular program being evaluated. This needs to be clarified. In fact, there is a need to carefully review/define clearly how the authors use methods (vis-a-vis methodology). In fact, later in the manuscript a much sharply defined aim is presented “This  paper focused on the description of methodology so here we are providing key facilitators and barriers . Our findings show several facilitators and barriers in implementation of the programme and offer some recommendations to improve the programme”. This has not been presented as an aim, but the results are organised along the framework of identifying facilitators and barriers for a particular programme, but the aims stated in the background are at a much higher level of abstraction. 
- Generally speaking, the context in a reasonably well-defined intervention are constituted by the entities, actors, relationships, elements etc in the immediate-distant environment/setting of the intervention, which are not part of the intervention itself, but have an effect on it. The section titled “context of the programme being evaluated” appears to be a mix of both the intervention elements and factors in the immediately environment of the intervention. Perhaps the authors meant to describe the setting within which the intervention was implemented? 
- Authors mention a “realistic approach for programme evaluation” under “Desigbubg the evaluation and procedures”. It is unclear if they are referring to the “realist approach” (also called realistic evaluation) by Pawson & Tilley. If this is the case, their framework and approach does not appear to fit within the iterative inquiry process of realist evaluation, where evaluators begin with an initial programme theory (kind of a middle range theory) and use hypothetical frames (context-mechanism-outcome configurations) to build an insight into what works, for whom and under what conditions. This needs major clarification. 
- Various issues with methods that need to be addressed (1) Case study as a “method”: Case study can be adopted as a methodology, a method and even appleid as a tool (albeit various critiques exist on these). The authors list case study as a method along with FGDs and interviews. Later in the results and discussion, it is unclear, how many case studies were conducted and how. Typically, case study method itself makes use of observation, interviews, and/or various other qualitative data collection methods. The section on methods requires greater clarity so that the reader may understand how these were used. (2) recruitment of participants as per their “availability” needs further explanation. How were they approached? Where were these FGDs/interviews/case studies conducted? etc. 
- The results summarise a list of facilitators and barriers. It is unclear how/where the “case studies” were used in the framing of the results. Moreover, there is insufficient contextual information to the reader to understand some of the results. For eg. what were the typical profiles of the study respondents. Perhaps the sharing of the coding tree and illustrating some of the facilitators and barriers with representative verbatim quotations may help, but a conceptual problem is that the study findings are looking at much more of the tangible program elements (like infrastructure, Human Resource availability etc.) whereas the underlying social processes that brought these about are not reported/inquired upon. 
- The section on Discussion and lessons learned needs careful grounding within the results of the study findings. Several generic statements that are not specific to their study findings are made. For eg. the first line “This article outlines and discusses the methodological framework used for the programme evaluation and provided the insights into practical consideration required for conducting such evaluations in community settings.” The authors’ claim of discussing “…THE (capitalisation mine) methodological framework used for programme evaluation” is not clearly understandable. They do not present a particular/previously unreported methodological framework. Further another example “This evaluation suggested that the findings need to be contextualized in terms of mental health program strategy, and the framework enabled us to discuss the relevance of this evaluation to the practice of mental health care in the country”. These have not been clearly identified in their results. Although these statements are indeed pertinent, it is unclear how they have emerged from the current study. Claims further down on completing the study in “…in a very quick time”, “…(adding) vital contextual framework around it” are all similarly lacking in grounding within the study findings, as well as being overly generic in nature. 
- The use of methodological triangulation and investigator triangulation comes up late in discussion. How this was applied and what it yielded is only asserted to in the discussion without providing details of their application in the results. 

Minor/discretionary comments
- Review Use of definite article in the title to refer to “the community based programme” which has not been introduced/qualified before. 
- Framing program evaluation as an “essential organisational practice in public health” while definitely acceptable intellectually, in order to ground this in public health practice, this requires quite some background and/or qualification. Program evaluation is often (in practice) an after-thought and is not rooted in various healthcare organisations’/health systems’ organisational practice framework. Moreover, organisational practice as a concept has its origins within organisational sciences, wherein most definitions allude to historicity, setting of norms over time and organisational culture elements. These require a bit of discussion while framing it within public health/helathcare organisations in my opinion. In its current form, the article uncritically asserts this.
