Firstly, I thank you the Director of Institute of Public Health, Bangalore for inviting me to this year’s Annual Day Oration.  Today, I would like to share my views on health, education and livelihoods for Adivasis through forest rights.  For the last 24 years, I have been working with the Solega people in Chamarajnagar (district). After completing my education, for 3 years, I was involved in community-based (social)work at the Vivekananda Girijana Kalyana Kendra (at BR Hills, Chamarajnagar).  Later, I have been working with the Ashoka Trust for research in Ecology & Environment (ATREE) for the last 21 years as a researcher on various aspects including forest conservation, harvesting of minor forest produce such as honey, *Antawaala* (Soapnut), *Nelli* (Indian Gooseberry) etc. Today one of the topics I would like to share today with you is campaign (of the *Solega)* in Chamarajnagar for obtaining livelihoods, education and health through forest rights act. Overall in Karnataka, there are 50 scheduled tribes, of which in Chamarajnagar, there are three forest-dependent Adivasi communities: Betta Kuruba, Jenu Kuruba and Solega. These communities are mainly dependent on 4 protected areas: Bandipur and BRT Tiger Reserves, Maleya Mahadeshwara and Kaveri Wildlife Sanctuaries. We live in and around these forest areas in our district. Since I am a Solega person myself and I am involved in research and (social) work with the Solega community, I will focus on the Solega. Let us discuss about the Forest Rights Act, 2006, which is a very important for the life of our people. This Act is very important for us, because say about 40 years back, there were no “Sanghatane” (social movements). Our people would fear/run away at the sight of “outsiders” and we were subject to atrocities/oppression. In that context, Dr. Sudarshan started the Vivekananda Girijana Kalyana Kendra (in 1981), and focused on education, health, community organisation. As the community organisation work moved ahead, there was an emphasis on education, not only for the Solega, but also for other Adivasi communities from Karnataka. Hence, a lot of work has been done on raising awareness and motivation. Now, we have our own Sanghathane (social movements), for which he too has supported. For example, the Zilla Budakattu Girijana Abhivruddhi Sangha and the Taluk Budakattu Girijana Abhivruddhi Sangha (District & Taluk Tribal/Girijana Development Collective/Organisation), these are all community-based organisations and I am working as the Secretary of the district Sangha and working across the district. I am also a Coordinator & Senior Research Associate at ATREE’s field station in BRT field station. In my 24 years of work and research experience, I have found that the forest rights act is a very important for Adivasi life. As you may know, in Karnataka there are originally 12 Adivasi groups which are all forest-dependent. They have lived for centuries in these forest areas. These are the Solega, Jenu Kuruba, Betta Kuruba, Yerawa, Malekudiya, Siddhi, Koraga, Gowdalu, Asalaru, Iruliga, Kudiya…these are communities which are living around and are dependent on the forest areas of the Western Ghats. Other than this, there are people included in the ST list who are not necessarily depending on forests and are living in cities. We dont consider them as Adivasi. Those who live in forests are the original Adivasi communities. Importantly, I feel that in Chamarajnagar the work on Forest Rights Act is going well. But, before that I feel I should introduce the Solega to you all. Who are the Solega people? In newspapers and media, you may have seen about Solegaru. We largely live in and around BR Hills, MM Hills  and Kaveri forest areas. We have a special relationship with the Biligirirangaswamky temple and the Maleya Mahadeshwara temple. We believe the Lord Maleya Mahadeshwara created us, he is our creator; similarly if we take Biligirirangana Betta (BR Hills) we believe the Lord Biligiriranganatha married Pusumaale (one of us). Hence we have been living here for centuries. If you see, there are Megalithic burial sites dated to around 800 BC further testifying to our living here for centuries. The Solega and the forest share a special relationship comparable to the child-mother relationship, the relationship between a mother and her child. We have that kind of a relationship (with the forest). How this impacts us is visible on a daily basis or during festivals. We Solega are nature worshippers, meaning that we hold the environment around us as sacred. We worship animals. For eg. we worship tigers as Huliveerappa, elephant as Aanedevaru, Sloth Bear as Karadidevaru/bear-god, we worship wild boar as Handidevaru, we worship Kaati/Bison (Gaur). So, there is this special relationship between us and the animals/forests. It is not that we are just “living” here. We view the forest around us as sacred. We have songs about the animals, eg about the Elephant, the tiger and we worship them. Among the Solega, we have five clans. All these clans worship (the animals and the forests). Not only animals, we also worship trees. In our culture, we worship Nerale (Jamun/Syzigium tree), Sampige (Magnolia/Champaka tree) etc. The Jamun tree for example is held to be very sacred; there are also songs about the tree. For example, in our culture, these kinds of songs worshipping tress and animals, and forest produce such as Nelli, honey, lichen, antwaala, seege...similarly, soppu and forest fruits are also gathered and we live like this. I feel that the special relationship between teh Solega is not only with trees and animals through worship but in fact celebrate and worship the entire life forms (biodiversity). This is our culture. Not only among the Solega but among the entire Adivasi life you can see this. I feel, for eg. the Rotti Habba, the harvesting festival where we sing teh Gorukana song….Goru Goru Kaa, Gorukana….in our language, this is the sound of the evergreen forest. We call the Spider as Goruka in the Solega language. When there is total silence one can hear the sound of the (weaving) spider. If one carefully watches, you can watch the dance of the spider (as it weaves its web). Based on this, we have woven our songs. These kind of songs and stories constitute our culture. For example, Doddasampige nannodeya, kaatu kaapaadi madagooneyya, Goru Goru Kaa, Gorukana….Goru Goru Kaa, Gorukana…We ask God, protect us, we worship you…this is our culture. Another example is songs about birds….Manna tegay tegay Kannaki maava, aralu kaddella mannakki goodu….      


So that was just an illustration of our songs about birds. Similarly, we have songs on trees as well. This is our knowledge. For eg. (another line in the Gorukana song) Mattiya mara, gorukanaade….like this we describe trees in our songs. This is an illustration of our Solega culture (with respect to trees and animals). More importantly, the Solega’s life is a simple one. This is our simplicity. The aspiration is not for pomp and show (for others). Ours is a “sharing system”. This means to share and eat with others. Among the Solega, what we grow, we like to share among others and eat. If there is something cooked at home, the norm is to share it with neighbours. Otherwise, if I have grown jowar/ragi (local millets), that too I will share with others, there is often exchange of produce across land-owners and sharing. This is part of our culture. This represents the kind of value systems/culture from which we come. “The Solega does not have knowledge” this kind of views sometimes held are wrong because we have deep knowledge a bout hte forest, about life here. From an outside view, outsiders often say “they have not yet come to mainstream/civilization, they are yet to “deveop” they say. In my opinion, I assert that this is wrong. Civilisation after all means that a person (aspires) for being “well”, live “well” in his environment, eat “well”, sleep “well”…these we hold as  important. It does not mean that someone wearing a certain kind of clothes or adornments. Civilisation also means human values. There are important values that we (Solega) hold. Mutual respect, emphasis on sharing food/produce. These values are important, especially mutual respect. We think like this when we talk of Solega/Adivasi rights. 

Now, I would like to share about the Scheduled Tribes And Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition Of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, also called Forest Rights Act (FRA). FRA is important for Adivasi, especially the Solega. As mentioned earlier, we live in and around 4 protected (forest) areas. IN these 4 areas, there are our *podus* (Solega settlements). We live in these podus. Living in these podus, as you konw, when the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 came into effect, BR Hills got declared as a wildlife sanctuary. Similarly, Bandipur was declared as a tiger reserve. Mahadeshwara betta became a wildlife sanctuary. As these announcements happened, there was a ban on collection of forest produce such as honey, Nelli, antwaala kaayi, seege….these kind of forest produce was the basis for our life. Along with these bans, 30-40 years back, our people have been evicted/uprooted from their homes and podus. Some podus have been relocated within forests while others have been relocated to the margins/outside forests. These kinds of acts we consider as injustice to our community. Recently in 2003, the Wildlife Protection Act was amended. After the amendment, there was a ban imposed on collection of minor forest produce in BRT, MM Hills and Kaveri forests. Nearly 50-60% of our cash income is from minor forest produce. So, this ban triggered a migration to other areas (due to livelihood stress) among the Solega, to Kerala, Kodagu, Tamil Nadu and other parts of Karnataka. Migration creates an obstacle for continuing the cultural traditions, also education of children, and on health too. When you dont get cash income, these kind of effects happen. IN that context, from our social movement (Sangha), we demanded for the collection of the minor forest produce. But they cited the amendment and refused. What we did is that all of us from the district got together and organised an agitation at the district headquarters and brought it to the notice of the state government. Several times with the assistance of organisations like VGKK and ATREE, we have given representations to the forest minister, Chief minister, other ministers by meeting them in person. Despite these representations, these rights were not granted to us. They all cited the wildlife act and refused. In one sense, this was an assault on our life and livelihoods. There was not even an alternative employment offered by the government (at the time). I am sharing this to show how important the forest rights act (which came later) is for us. At that time, we were continuously involved in agitations of this kind, but they were all in vain. But then the forest rights act, 2006 came. When the Act came, we all decided at the district Sangha that we must take the community-level forest rights. We first submitted an application for community rights to secure minor forest produce collection (due to the ban). This was in 2008. After that, we started applying for land rights. So, it is not that easy to get rights (despite there being an Act). However, in Chamarajnagar, the Adivasi leaders took a good decision. The Act provides for 4 levels of committees for forest rights: village, sub-division, district and state level forest rights committees. In all these commitees, we ensured that Solega membership is there. This is what hte leaders emphasised on to begin with. Probably, in Karnataka state, in other regions, there are hardly any Adivasis in these committees, may be 1 or 2 here and there. But in Chamarajnagar, we ensured that we are all there. At that time the DC and District Tribal WElfare Officer all helped. Not that I or we did this all by ourselves. They all cooperated and we ensured that our membership in all committees is secured. Because, for implementing the Act, it is only possible only if we (Adivasis) are well represented. If there are others (non-Adivasis) it will be difficult to implement the act. So from that view, we became members. After becoming members, we conducted meetings in every single Podu. We formed forest rights committees in each and every Podu. After that the committees were all trained (on FRA). After the training to all committee members, how to fill up the forest rights application, we gave full information on securing rights…this has happened continuously since it began in 2008 without a pause. Yet, even up to 2010 not a single kind of forest right was granted in our district. Because the forest department raised objections several times that hte wildlife protection act bans this (despite there being the FRA). Later, after an empowered committee from the central government visited bangalore, at the time our leaders went and met them, the implementation began. At the time, the DC was Chakravarthy Mohan, he took a lot of interest, “I must give these forest rights” he strongly felt and we held several discussion with him. After these discussions and after 3-4 years of such discussions and then followed by again things remaining pending for months to years, this continued. Later we produced precedents of forest rights and implementation of FRA from Odisha, and then from Andhra Pradesh and further built up pressure “Why you are not granting rights despite these precedents and the FRA?” We demanded. Other than that, what we did is we involved all the people’s representatives in the district across political parties. WE told them “you must get our hakku Patra (rights document)” we demanded, let it be MLA, MP and officers. Every month we would meet them 2-3 times a month. Our single aim was that the Act should be implemented fully in our district. Hence, in 2010 for the first time (in our district), about 1000 families got land rights across these 4 protected (forest) areas. So we go these land rights. But truly, what we call “community rights” that is very important. Yes, we got land rights, but these achievements were not of one day or merely because of the Act. They are the results of continuous agitation/ struggles over years. So, 2020 families across the district till date have obtained land rights. This is because, our district and taluk Sangha, VGKK, ATREE all of these have cooperated and facilitated this. When we work in this, all the work is voluntary. Nobody is paid for this, be it for filling applications, or appearing for agitations, these are all voluntary work. All of us worked volunatarily. Each of us would go to thes meetings and all of this was voluntary work. SO, we finally did secure our lands rights. In land rights, if we consider BR Hills, this became a tiger reserve in 2011. At that time when it was declared as a tiger reserve we organised a campaign/agitation against this. Because, we feared that making this a tiger reserve could result in further eviction/uprooting of our people and our podus. This is the very principle of creating tiger reserve elsewhere. We appealed against this. Yet, this was not considered and the government went ahead and notified BR Hills as a tiger reserve. When we see this, be it BR Hills, MM Hills wildlife sanctuaries and forests, wherever land rights have been granted it its through constant agitation and struggles. Another special thing is that across Karnataka, the highest number of land rights granted is in Chamarajnagar. 


This is not just an empty claim. It is a source of fulfilment for us that our community has got its rights. It is not important that we stood first in the state. Rather, we worked together to ensure that our community got their rights - that is important. So we have obtained these title deeds (for the lands) under FRA. We believe this is very important for (our) health as well. When we say health, we dont just think of “disease” and “medicines”. For us, land is important. Why we stress upon title deeds (for the land) is because only about 40% of us have land. 60% of us dont have land, they are landless even though they would like to practice farming. If this title deeds as per our rights is granted, then we can do farming on this land. We can grow different varieties of foods crops, horticultural crops and fruits. These will be possible if we have land and this is mainly for subsistence. About 40 years ago, Solega were practicing shifting cultivation. 3-4 years in one piece of land and then shift. Now we practice “settled agriculture”. In this too, most of the farming is for eating (subsistence agriculture). We might share it with others in the community but mainly for “self-consumption”. Selling farm produce is rare. This is our practice. Hence, what we grow, the “soppugalu” (green leafy vegetables), ragi/jowar (millets), avare (broad bean), togari (pigeon pea), or other crops, we use it for food. The land rights becomes a way of maintaining health (nutrition). We grow good food, and we eat them and our health improves. This is how forest rights act helps. This is what I feel. This can also be viewed from the angle of food security and health. For us, land is very important, for every Adivasi community. That’s how we see things about forest rights. Then, another thing that I believe is that strengthening our farming improves our nutrition. Even in soppu (green leafy vegetables), we grow 10-15 varieties. IN our lands, we call it Kumbala (pumpkin). IN that too we have 7-8 varieties. We grow 15-20 varieties of beans. In green leaves (soppu), we have several varieties. All these are sources of proteins and nutrients for our people. Hence, this Act (FRA) is very important (for health). We all believe that the Act (FRA) is important for our health. For securing our lands, despite teh Act we have struggled over 15 years. Further, there are also community forest rights (CFR). This means that the minor forest produce such as honey, nelli, Antawaala, Seege, lichen and such other products, about 10-12 varieties that are of interest to us are there in the forest. We collect these. We depend on these too for our livelihoods. On October 2, 2011, we successfully obtained title deeds for CFR for 25 forest rights committees in BRT Tiger Reserve. This is an achievement because this is within a tiger reserve. Nearly people in 30 podus are covered by this CFR….we call our villages as podus. In BR Hills, there are actually 61 podus. So half of the podus have received their community rights. So, there are now rights to gather honey, paase (lichen), nelli, seege, antawaala….and other forest produce, there are opportunities now to harvest them. From this, nearly the people among us who are landless, the remaining 60% can now gain livelihoods by this harvesting and sale of minor forest produce. Also, the remaining 40% who are landed also dont have too much land. Few have 0.5, others 0.25 and still others have 1 acre. People have spread misinformation about forest rights act. That because of the act every tribal will get 10 acres. This was a malicious rumour that was spread against our interest. The act only provided title deeds for how much land an individual was already farming. This was a misconception among outsiders, forest departments, other departments. But what has been granted is at the most 1-2 acres. But, due to the securing of the rights for harvesting minor forest produce, now we have again restarted this. Because of this, there is income for some of us. See for eg. honey that we sell for say about 220/kg, from that sale whatever profits accrue is directly going towards subsistence and daily living of tribal families. It is not that this is making us (Adivasi) rich. This is almost like a daily wage. This goes towards food and living. This is very important for us forest-dwellers. If we have to live in forest, forest rights are very important for our life and living. Because of our constant agitation and struggles by so many leaders and others, in BRT tiger reserve region till 2021, overall 45 community rights we have obtained. This covers 40-70 podus in teh tiger reserve. This now means that we can harvest minor forest produce. Similarly, in MM Hills area, about 28 forest rights committees have obtained community rights. There too, Solega live in 40-50 podus. This now means they have access to enter forest around them. Similarly, in Kaveri wildlife sanctuary 4 forest rights committees have obtained community rights. There too, (collection of minor forest produce) was banned. Now they can collect. But if you see Bandipur tiger reserve, even to this day (despite FRA), community rights have not been obtained. We continue our agitation there (for securing community forest rights). There 33 podus/haadis of Adivasis there. We are demanding our community rights. Already in 1974, it was declared as a tiger reserve and adivasi communities at the time were uprooted from inside and brought to the margins. So they have rejected our applications. Every single day, they are thrusting “cases” on Adivasis entering forest (for minor forest produce collie ton). So we demand our forest rights and we continue our struggles there. Similarly, in mart other districts too, wherever there is an opportunity to collect minor forest produce, the Adivasi communities have not yet been granted rights. There too, from our Sanghatane (social movement) we continue the agitations and I too am personally involved. So, this is a struggle at multiple levels (from podus to state level) and we work at all these levels. From this, we build community resources which is very important. Be it honey, be it our food, these are after all accomplishing health. So, securing community forest rights has helps maintain our health. The herbs that we find in the forest…there are many varieties of herbs in the forest. We use some of these with medicinal properties. When we consider health, we also have several traditions that are relevant. So, when we think of health, the role of forest and our environment too is important. People often look at forest/environment as separate from health. Only if there is forest, we are healthy. For us Adivasis, without forests there is no health. We believe that forests keep us healthy. They are sources of medicinal plants, we get good quality air, fruits….because of this our health is maintained. We also have several traditions. Among the Solega, when we talk of health, there are several traditions that we follow. Taking (modern) medicines is one way. Among us, we also use medicinal plants. They often work. When they dont we also do “Kani shaastra”. That too is our tradition. For eg. if someone has a stomach ache, why did is come? Is there a possession by a “bad spirit”/energy, we try to find out what it is and where it has come from. This is our way along us. There is also Gunji shaasthra. This is done in darkness, and our elders try to “find out” about a disease. If someone has a fever, what is the reason, why did it come, had he gone to the forest, his travels…all these can be (loosely) compared to what you call “counselling”…we do like this…..in counselling, this kind of an interrogation happens - why it happened, where he had gone…this is like a counselling technique that we use. The person suffering the illness and their caregiver also sit together and the Gunji shaastra happens with all of them. What happens is that the person experiences a relief of the “feeling” inside him. Finally, when the “Gunji” is beaten (out), he says “I have driven away the spirits. You be brave…have courage”. At the time, the negative feelings in the Solega go away. This is one of the important ways we do this. Also “devarannu kelodu”…we go to our temple and we “ask God” here. When we “ask”, the God speaks (through our elder) about the illness. He says “you be brave. I willl relieve you of yoru illness. come after a few weeks”. He says. Some people go for up to 3 weeks. After this, he says “dont be scared” and ash and lemon are given and he says “everythign will be ok. I am there with you. you have my blessings”. Here too, if you look in a scientific way, this works similar to counselling. The weekly follow-up where “how are you? are you improving” kind of questions. Changes are assessed weekly. This is how it works in our tradition. The negative feelings that build up within, we remove them through our traditional ways. Similar traditions are there in several adivasi commujnities. Hence when we seek these traditions, they are also a spiritual way of addressing disease. We connect with the spiritual aspects of health through these practices. We also beleive that this helps with curing disease as well. We also visit hospitals (for modern medicine). Not only depending on our traditions, we also visit hospitals. Even in Forest rights act, there is something called “cultural rights”. That too we are securing in our area. From ATREE, along with Nitin Rai, we mapped 489 cultural sites across BRT Tiger Reserve…there is a story of each of these sites. 


Worship happens at all these sites. These are clearly demarcated sites (historically)…”territories”. This is an amazing illustration of the strong cultural bond between us and the forests. Finally we have these rights to access these sites. The rights allow us to go to these sites, worship. We also have rights to fish in waterbodies in the forests. We can fish there. We have taken these under the forest rights act. From this, we can get proteins. Eating fish is common, since we Solega eat both vegetarian and non-vegetarian food products, so fish is very helpful (for protein). Another illustration of how the forest rights act is linked with health. There is also Education. Since Education is also one of the things to discuss today, I bring up life-skills education. Knowledge about the forest. If we have to live in the forest, we need to know how to harvest forest products, how to escape and protect from some animals, and other knowledge about the ways of the forest…this is part of our traditional education. This we integrate into our interaction and every time we go to the forest is a way of learning these. Through smell we know (about some animals)…there is pugmarks…we listen to birds’ alarm calls…these are all our education….so living in the forest means we need to know about the forest….if you live in the city, you need to know about how to get around there…if we live in teh forest, we may not need detailed knowledge of life in cities. If we live there, then of course that is meaningful. So this (traditional view of education) is also helpful (for us). Forest rights act is again important for this too (by accessing forests). We must apply our traditional knowledge. We have rich ecological knowledge. About forests, about animals, about birds….these kind of knowledge we experience here…about the basic amenities of daily life for our people, what hte forest rights act says it hat drinking water, road, electricity supply….for eg. in so many podus like Gombegallu, Nellikadaru, the roads are not good, there is no drinking water or there is insufficient housing. These basic amenities can be facilitated by forest rights act. Only when drinking water is proper will the person be healthy. Only if there is proper transport/roads, there will be health. With electricity supply, health will improve. All these as well as good quality housing, good sleep, health and wellness will be possible. All these we see as health, but can also be seen as Education. Now, due to the Sangha (Solega collective) and the cooperation of many others, since 10 years, those Solega children who are completing their education, we conduct motivation workshops. Every year, we do 2-3 such workshops. In the Solega, 200 children are in college now from PUC to masters degrees, we help all of them. Similarly, we try to source scholarships through external donations for our children. For eg. ATREE, established by Dr. Kamaljit Bawa, similarly VGKK, there is Siddappa Shetty from Samarthanam Trust…from them we source scholarships for our children…every year we are trying to strengthen education among teh Solega. In the last 10 years, we and others form the Sanghatane (collective) are working. Through such efforts, our education progress improves (we hope). Regarding Education, wherever needed we also help with college admission (for Solega children). These kind of activities are ongoing. However, it is not that as soon as one is educated, there will be job (for us). There are job reservations, but many other community groups (other than Adivasi) have been included in the scheduled tribe and this is creating injustice for us. Because of this, forest-dependent Adivasis like us are getting deprived of employment (despite education). These issues are there….so, coming to life-skill education, what this means is for eg. how to climb a tree, how to harvest honey (from the beehive), what is the ways and means in forest like, these are important we believe. For eg. life skill education for honey harvesting….(sings a song)

“Hey brother, there is a beehive at Kembare bare (a rocky location)” (2 times)
“Hey brother, lets plan to harvest the honey” (2 times)
“Hey brother, Lo, our dear bear uncle approaches” (2 times)

These kind of songs include life-skills on where the honey is, how to climb through song. Warnings about animals to look out for are built into the song. This is our life skill…similar is knowledge of the soppus (green leafy vegetables) that we eat. There are songs about these too. 

For eg. the song “Cut and eat the Karavaadi soppu”. The song goes on about how to cut the Karavaadi soppu, or Honnagonne soppu…there are songs about these….so songs and food join in our tradition. We have songs on every life form, bees, animals, birds. 

So these are some views that I wished to share with you all and so…for the opportunity to share this with you all, I thank Dr. Upendra, Dr. Prashanth and others from IPH, as well as Dr. Devadasan, the President (founder)…I offer my gratitude to them for this platform. Further, for over past 5 years, along with us, in BR Hills and in Chamarajnagar district, IPH has been involved in research work. In this work, they work together with us. Earlier too, we have had experience of working with IPH. And now, we have also taken up collaborative work with IPH on forest rights and health. We continue our work on forest rights with this support. We hope that such kids of (participatory) research may increase and hope that they will influence policies and these will be of use. IN health too, there are many possibilities of improving policy through research. IN this spirit of community participation, the IPH team has been working in BR Hills through their field station. We reaffirm our commitment to work with them in this. I wish to thank them for this. Finally, my message is that when we look at (Adivasi) health or from (Adivasi) health perspective, let us not just say “he is ill”or “he does not have hygiene”….let us expand our perspective….his rights too are important….only if he has rights, access to the forest…for the Solega the access to forest is important….only if our forest, our environment, our tradition is there….only then will there be forest conservation…..not enough to just provide (health) services…forests and health too are needed…..along with health services..these are my views. Another appeal to you all is that those among you who wish to do research on (Adivasi) health, wildlife conservation, (Adivasi) socio-economic aspects, please work collaboratively with (Adivasi) community-based organisations if you want your work to be (socially) impactful. Such work will result in more effective programs and may create change/transformation (in Adivasi lives). There is no use of individual research by one or two people all by themselves. This is my view. With this, I thank you all and the Institute of Public Health for this opportunity to share my views.
