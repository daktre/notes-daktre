(Unedited/unreviewed summary of the group work)

The group began by reflecting that what they were talking about was _informal_ in the sense of being organic, relational and evolving, but not informal in the sense of being casual or unaccountable. The activities that make up this work still need to be funded; they still require “fans” and resources to drive them. The relationships themselves are not informal arrangements that sit outside responsibility. When someone asked, “By informal, what do you mean?”, the response was that these are dense, ongoing relationships between policy actors, embedded researchers, and others – but held together more by trust and practice than by formal governance structures.

  

Participants encouraged colleagues to read the paper that had been written to describe the “Cajunas of Africa,” noting that there had already been a couple of annual dialogues connected to that work. One person said they liked the language Gilles had used: all the things they keep talking about in, for example, the Western Cape – relationships with policy actors, the role of embedded researchers, the everyday work together – all of these activities taken together form something larger. “If you put them in a warehouse,” someone said, “how you describe that warehouse is what we’re calling a learning site.”

  

This led to the question: _Who owns the relationships in such a learning site?_ The answer offered was “everyone.” All the actors in the learning site co-own the relationships. But this ownership does not come from classic governance or formal accountability structures. Instead, it is built on relationships and trust. There may be no formal governance board, but there is accountability — a kind of _relational accountability_.

  

Long-term relationships, it was argued, only happen where this relational accountability is real. You do not get durable relationships if people fail to produce what they said they would, or if researchers go off and pursue their own agendas instead of the system’s priorities. If researchers were to follow only their own agendas rather than the health system’s agendas, that would fracture the accountability that sustains the learning site. This, they argued, is exactly what they are trying to avoid, and it is also how communication within the learning site works in practice.

  

One participant acknowledged that calling this a “learning site” is, in some ways, an academic way of describing what they are already doing – or what they _should_ be doing – in a way that others, in other places, can also recognise as similar work. Several people agreed: “We’re also doing the same.” Yet, at present, there is no obvious reason or mechanism for people who do this in India and people who do it in, say, South Africa to meet or exchange experiences. Everyone is “lost in some local thing.” Naming and describing the learning site is a way of lifting such work up and saying, “This is not just local. This is important.”

  

Someone then raised the core question: _Why are we even bothering to describe or define it in this way?_ The group offered two main reasons.

  

First, there are multiple activities inside a learning site, and they all require resources. To secure and sustain those resources, there needs to be some kind of “packaging” – a way of showing what these activities are, how they hang together, and why they should be funded. Everyone in the room agreed these activities are important; the issue is how to represent and support them.

  

Second, from a research pathway perspective – and here the Kenyan experience was evoked – there is an explicit intention to learn and document. The learning site itself becomes a research process. But doing research _within_ this kind of relational, emergent governance is difficult. In Kenya, they had to write a research protocol and submit it for ethics approval, even though they had no fixed research questions, no pre-specified tools, and no rigid timeline. They went to the ethics committee effectively saying, “We have no questions and we have no tools. The questions will emerge, and we will develop the tools as we go.” Ethics reviewers understandably asked, “What are you saying?” It took a lot of struggle to describe the learning site in a way that could be understood and approved as a legitimate research process.

  

At this point, another question was raised: _If this is a learning site, how do you show that learning is actually happening?_ Usually, when you learn, you’re examined – but here there are no exams. The group responded that evidence of learning comes largely through documentation. As Jill pointed out, there are many small projects inside the learning site. Each of these has outputs that are examined, assessed, and evaluated in more conventional ways. There are “lots of little projects” with their own evaluations.

  

One concrete example given was the journal club. They had even evaluated the journal club itself – asking whether it was having any meaningful impact. Many similar activities inside the learning site can be tracked and assessed like regular research projects. What has _not_ been fully assessed, however, is the overarching approach: the overall mode of engagement. Sophie had earlier pointed out that the big question is whether this overarching approach of engagement has a long-term impact on building learning health systems. That part – the claim that the learning site as an approach contributes to developing a learning system – remains largely unevaluated. All the “stuff going on inside” is valued and measured like normal research projects, but the collective approach still lacks a robust evaluation.

  

This links to another recurring problem: when people outside ask, “So, what do you actually do?”, those involved in the learning site often end up launching into a long, messy explanation: “Oh, we build engagement; we re-create this phase; we do these meetings; we do that…”. To many, this sounds unfundable. Someone remarked that funders had told them directly, in effect, “This is not fundable.” Underneath, there is a politics here: the group is trying to _claim_ these complex, relational and institutional arrangements as legitimate objects for funding and investment. By describing them as a learning site, they hope to move them from the invisible, taken-for-granted background into something that can be recognised, valued, and resourced.

  

Linked to this is the question of time. Time costs money, and everyone is extremely busy. Why should people give time “just to have meetings”? Intuitively, many of them feel that things like the journal club “work.” That club has been running for 12 years, year after year. All of them are busy people, and yet they commit their scarce time to it because they find it important and useful. The argument is that this shared, long-term time investment is a contribution to the learning site approach. It is one of the ways in which the learning system is sustained and strengthened.

  

Looking ahead, participants felt that if this idea really takes off, the main actors in the learning sites of the future may not primarily be academic researchers. Instead, they will be people already embedded in the systems – practitioners, managers, frontline workers – whose knowledge and experience can be brought to the foreground. The learning site would then create a ground on which their knowledge is recognised and cultivated.

  

As the session drew to a close, someone noted that time was up and that they needed to be back in another room (the “21st”). They asked who would like to remain engaged in moving this work forward and invited people to give their names, adding that no one should feel obliged. A smaller group would likely do some of the heavy lifting and then keep everyone else informed.

  

There was some light-hearted teasing about Opa, who, it was said, would be involved “whether he likes it or not” because “it’s kind of what he does.” He was jokingly labelled “the landing page” of the learning site, a label he apparently did not know he had. Nancy was also called a “landing page,” though she described herself as still ignorant and skeptical, interested but needing to learn more. Others responded that this was exactly what the learning site is for – that she should “learn the learning site.”

  

The group traded some “learning site jokes” and then turned back to terminology. Several felt that the word “approach” needed to be brought more explicitly into how they described this work. A learning site, they suggested, is not just a project or a physical place: it is a _mode_ and an _approach_ to engagement and research. They also emphasised that the geospatial dimension needed to be clearer. A learning site is geographically defined: it is rooted in a specific area, and that sense of geography is really important. Geography shapes relationships, opportunities, and risks.

  

Finally, they reflected on what might bring multiple learning sites together. The answer, they felt, is always relative. Each learning site is situated within a particular area and context, but across them there needs to be a shared willingness “to take a chance” – to work relationally, to build trust, to accept emergent questions and tools, and to commit scarce time and energy to long-term engagement.
