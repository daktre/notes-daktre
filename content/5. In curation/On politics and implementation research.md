I have writen before about how IR is potentilly a positive step towards locating public health problems beyond individuls within instituitons (see my [paper in IJMR on this](https://ijmr.org.in/beyond-describing-patterns-a-methodological-critique-of-health-equity-research-in-india/)) But as [Mathew George](https://schools.cukerala.ac.in/Dept/Faculty_Preview?Id=172) often says, this is not automatically teh case. IR can become quite banal, quite pedantic and techno-managerial espeically in the context of instituiotnal failures, governance or in the area of health inequities/health justice work. IN this context this recent paper 

**Bridging policy and implementation science: operationalising political and structural determinants for health policy implementation evaluation** ([link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43058-026-01137-0))

"Their paper proposes 8 dimensions for systematically identifying and reporting the political and structural determinants of health policy implementation, bringing issues such as actor power, institutional authority, policy instruments and jurisdiction into established implementation frameworks."

Potential limitation?  Political analysis risks becoming a checklist of factors to document rather than an approach to explain how policy processes unfold. How do ideas, interests and power relations interact? How do narratives shape which problems become politically actionable, whose knowledge counts, and which policy choices appear legitimate?

The authors acknowledge that further methodological and empirical development is needed. The challenge, however, is also conceptual: we need political analysis to explain implementation, not simply to describe its context.
