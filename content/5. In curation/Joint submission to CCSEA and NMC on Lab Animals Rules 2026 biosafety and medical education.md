## Executive summary

We ask CCSEA to withdraw the draft Rules in their current form and re-notify them after the amendments set out in Section 4 of the letter, and we ask the National Medical Commission to support those amendments in its own response. 

We welcome the move to severity-based classification and the explicit adoption of the 3Rs. however, the draft treats laboratory animal use mainly as a welfare question. Its biosafety provisions cover the biological agents that researchers choose to use, but does not cover the infections/pathogens that animals could be carrying. Particularly, the draft's exceptions and approval routes create a pathway for animals of unknown pathogen status into laboratories and their workforce:

1. Open-market sourcing in Rule 21(2): Livestock, farm animals, fish and birds may be bought from unregistered sources or the open market on IAEC approval alone, with quarantine but no pathogen screening. This coudl potentially  extend to any animal above lagomorphs.
2. Exemptions for wild and stray animals in Rules 5(2) and 5(3): An undefined "competent authority" may exempt wild-caught animals, and may exempt stray and feral animals in conditions of a disease threat
3. In-house approval (Rule 11 and Annexure I): IAECs alone approve mild and moderate protocols in all species, which include Risk Group I–II pathogen studies, and severe protocols in rodents, which include Risk Group III/IV studies.
4. Open and mobile establishments (Rule 2(i)) with containment required only for animals deliberately exposed to Risk Group III/IV agents.

There have been past instances oof laboratory-acquired zoonotic infections in India. Free-ranging dogs, which the draft may allow to enter laboratories, sit at the interface of people, livestock and wildlife and such animal housing establishments in the colleges could result in exchange of pathogens between animals with unknown pathogen status. 

India is building a National One Health Mission and a national network of BSL-3 laboratories to detect and contain these threats. Rules that lower the entry gate for animals of unknown pathogen status into ordinary laboratories works this purpose. 

We ask for the following:
1. a biological risk axis alongside the severity axis, 
2. a return to registered-source procurement, 
3. central review for any wild-caught or non-purpose-bred animal, and 
4. joint review with MoHFW, ICMR, MoEFCC and the National Institute of One Health.

Sir / Madam,

We write as public-health, medical, conservation and One Health organisations and researchers working in India. Our signatories include ........ We submit these comments in the public interest, and in response to the NMC public notice.

We acknowledge the intent of the draft. Classifying procedures as non-recovery, mild, moderate or severe according to pain and distress, rather than by the size of the animal, is a sound principle. So is the explicit adoption of Replacement, Reduction and Refinement. We support these elements and ask that they be retained.

Our concern is that the draft regulates laboratory animals as a welfare matter alone. It does not treat the movement of animals into laboratories as a biosafety matter. Where an animal comes from, what it may be carrying, and who handles it are questions of public health. On these questions, the draft weakens safeguards that has.

## 1. What the draft provides, and where it falls short

The draft was notified as G.S.R. 864(E), dated 30 September 2026, and supersedes both the 1968 and 1998 Rules. Some of its provisions strengthen biosafety: default prohibitions on wild and stray animals (Rule 5), negative-pressure housing for animals exposed to Risk Group III or IV agents (Rule 19(2)), and IBC approval for potential human pathogens (Schedule III, item 14). However, there remain important concerns outlined below:

### 1.1 Open-market procurement (Rule 21(2))

Rule 21(1) retains procurement from CCSEA-registered breeders, establishments or traders only. Rule 21(2) then allows that, where "livestock and farm animals (above level of Lagomorpha), fishes and birds" are not available from registered sources, they "may be procured from non-registered establishments or open market with prior approval of IAEC", subject to veterinarian-certified quarantine as prescribed by CCSEA guidelines.

- Birds and livestock are important hosts : Live-bird markets are a recognised interface for avian influenza, and livestock carry ticks implicated in various tick-borne infections Open-market sourcing of these animals is the opposite of what a One Health approach requires.
- **Ambigious scope** The draft says "above level of Lagomorpha" and htis can be read as extending the exception to any animal above rabbits and hares, including dogs, pigs and primates. 
- **Quarantine is not screening**: The rule requires veterinary certification and a quarantine period, both left to guidelines. It requires no testing for zoonotic pathogens.
- **Approval rests with the IAEC alone,** with no CCSEA or public-health input.

Relatedly, Rule 8(3) lets registered traders deal in animals "bred, procured and housed" without specifying where traders may procure from, and Rule 16(6) exempts traders who do not house animals from having an IAEC. A registered trader can therefore be a conduit for animals of unrecorded origin including those involved in wildlife trade/animals poached from the wild. 

### 1.2 Wild animals and non-human primates (Rules 5 and 6)

Rule 5(2)(a) bars animals taken from the wild "unless the Competent authority of the relevant department grants exemptions" on scientific justification. "Competent authority" is not defined in Rule 2 and should be no less than the Chief Wildlife Warden of the state/State board for wildlife. The exemption needs no CCSEA approval, and does not name any wildlife authority, and requires no health screening. Rule 5(2)(c) provides only for veterinary examination of injured or unwell animals, and even this can be exempted. This has to be aligned with the Scheduled species identified in the Wildlife Protection Act. 

### 1.3 In-house approval of mild and moderate protocols in all species (Rule 11 and Annexure I)

Rule 11(3) sends mild and moderate protocols, "including such experiments on all animals", to the IAEC alone, and states that "Such experiment shall not be referred to the Committee". Under Rule 11(2), CCSEA reviews only severe and non-recovery experiments on animals above rodents and lagomorphs. Annexure I classes "Studies involving Risk Group I–II pathogens" and routine blood collection as moderate, and Risk Group III/IV studies as severe. Together, these mean:

- infection studies with Risk Group II agents in primates, dogs or birds are approved in-house;
- severe Risk Group III/IV studies in rodents, the standard models for viruses such as KFD and Nipah, are also approved in-house, since Rule 11(2) escalates only experiments on larger animals;
- reporting to CCSEA under Rule 11(4) is retrospective.

### 1.6 Biosafety confined to deliberately used agents

The IBC requirement in Schedule III, item 14, applies to recombinant agents and potential human pathogens that investigators intend to use. Currently the draft does not addresse infections that animals may alredy be having. There is no requirement for health screening of incoming non-purpose-bred animals, occupational health surveillance of staff, or reporting of laboratory-acquired infections. The designated veterinarian under Rule 14 monitors animal well-being only. The draft makes no reference to the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, MoHFW or the National One Health Mission.

### 1.7 Consultation period

The notification allows thirty days for objections. Health, environment and forest departments have a direct stake and appear to not yet have been sufficiently engaged. Much longer consultation period is needed.

## 2. Why this is a pandemic-preparedness issue

Laboratories concentrate animals, people and aerosol-generating procedures in one place. If the animals arrive with unknown infections, laboratory staff become the first human hosts. 

### 2.1 The animals most easily sourced are recognised reservoirs

- **Bats**: Nipah virus RNA has been found in Indian *Pteropus medius*, *Rousettus* and *Pipistrellus* bats ([Mohandas et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiology/articles/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1493428/epub)). The May 2018 Kerala outbreak had 23 confirmed cases and 21 deaths, and 19.2% of bats sampled tested positive ([Chatterjee, 2018, PMC6231092](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6231092)). 
- **Primates, small mammals and ticks:** KFD transmission involves several tick species and hosts including wild rodents, shrews, monkeys and birds. Human spillover risk is highest in agro-forestry mosaics where moist evergreen forest has been converted ([Purse et al., 2023](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-04188168)). 
- **Free-ranging dogs:** India's free-ranging dogs attack at least 80 wildlife species, 31 of them threatened, and nearly half of reported incidents occur in and around protected areas ([Home, Bhatnagar and Vanak, 2018](https://archived.atree.org/node/1952)). Dogs are therefore a bridge between wildlife, livestock and people, and the principal source of human rabies. Rule 5(3) would allow stray and feral dogs to be used under exemption during "serious threats" to human or animal health.

### 2.3 Spillover is a social and ecological process

Our research on KFD, co-produced with affected communities, district health officials and forest departments in the Western Ghats, shows that exposure follows livelihoods, land use and access to care ([Purse et al., 2020](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/527852/); [Asaaga et al., 2023](https://archived.atree.org/node/3725); [PLOS Global Public Health, 2022](https://journals.plos.org/globalpublichealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgph.0000075)). An informal market for laboratory animals would create new livelihoods around capturing and moving wildlife. It would draw on the same forest-edge communities who already carry the burden of zoonoses, and expose them further.

### 2.4 The draft works against India's preparedness investment

The National One Health Mission was approved in 2022 and is anchored at the National Institute of One Health, Nagpur ([Office of the PSA](https://www.psa.gov.in/innerPage/psa-initiatives-covid/one-health/4053/4053)). Under it, a national network of BSL-3 laboratories has been created and surveillance of wildlife, migratory birds and slaughterhouses is under way. ICMR-NIV's director has said the urgency came from COVID-19, when only NIV had BSL-4 capacity ([Medical Dialogues](https://health.medicaldialogues.in/health-topics/zoonotic-disease-surveillance-expanded-in-india-under-national-one-health-mission-150340)). On one hand, building high-containment capacity for surveillance here while allowing animals of unknown pathogen status into ordinary laboratories approved in-house may be ill-adviced. 

## 3. Implications for medical colleges and medical education

3 points for NMC and for medical colleges:

1. **Teaching is a listed purpose under Rule 4(6)** ("higher education, or professional training for the acquisition, maintenance or improvement of vocational skills"), with no requirement that replacement alternatives be exhausted first. We submit that Rule 4(6) be read with an explicit provison as follows: animals shall not be used for teaching where a validated simulator, virtual or computer-assisted module, manikin, or ethically sourced cadaveric or clinical material can meet the learning objective.
2. **The risks in Section 1 apply to teaching laboratories:** A medical college that obtains animals under the Rule 21(2) open-market route, or under a Rule 5(2) or 5(3) exemption, exposes undergraduate and postgraduate students, laboratory attendants and housekeeping staff to animals of unrecorded health status.
3. **Institutional capacity is uneven:** Shifting approval of all mild and moderate protocols in all species to the IAEC (Rule 11(3)) assumes every college's IAEC can assess biological risk. Many cannot, and the draft requires no member with biosafety or medical microbiology expertise (Section 1.4). NMC may wish to seek a transition period, model SOPs and a training requirement for IAEC members in colleges under its purview.

We request that NMC to place its own consolidated response in the public domain, and to encourage CCSEA to publish all comments received in both windows. 

## 4. Amendments requested

We request that the draft be withdrawn and re-notified with the following changes:

1. **Rule 21(2): delete the open-market route,** or at minimum (a) remove birds and livestock, (b) delete the parenthetical "(above level of Lagomorpha)" so the exception cannot reach dogs, pigs or primates, (c) require CCSEA approval rather than IAEC approval, and (d) require pathogen screening, specified in the Rule, in addition to quarantine.
2. **Rule 8(3): require traders to source only from registered breeders,** and to keep provenance records for every animal; reconsider the IAEC exemption for traders in Rule 16(6).
3. **Rule 5(2): define "competent authority"** as the Chief Wildlife Warden acting jointly with CCSEA, and require documented pathogen screening and quarantine for any wild-caught animal. Make sourcing of primates from purpose-bred, self-sustaining colonies mandatory, not merely subject to review under Rule 5(2)(d). Add bats as a category requiring CCSEA approval for any use.
4. **Rule 5(3): restrict the stray and feral exemption** to non-invasive studies of the animals'  health, define stray, feral and community animals, and bar their transfer into laboratory colonies. Correct the drafting of the clause.
5. **Rule 11 and Annexure I: add a biological-risk axis.** Send to CCSEA, regardless of severity class, any protocol involving (a) animals not purpose-bred by a registered breeder, (b) non-human primates or bats, or (c) Risk Group II or higher agents in any animal above rodents and lagomorphs, and Risk Group III/IV agents in any species. Make the IBC approval in Schedule III, item 14, a condition of IAEC approval in the body of the Rules.
6. **Rule 16: add a member with biosafety or medical microbiology expertise to the IAEC,** with a vote on any protocol involving infectious agents or non-purpose-bred animals. Give the specialist co-opted under Rule 16(5) a vote.
7. **Rule 2(i): delete "a place that is not wholly enclosed or covered and mobile facilities".** Under Rule 19, require enclosed premises, separate quarantine for incoming animals, vector control, and carcass and waste disposal compliant with the Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016.
8. **New rule on occupational health and reporting:** Require health surveillance of staff handling non-purpose-bred animals, pre-exposure vaccination where available (rabies; KFD where relevant), and mandatory reporting of laboratory-acquired infections to the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme.
9. **Make the Rules a One Health instrument:** Finalise them jointly with MoHFW (NCDC, ICMR), MoEFCC and the National Institute of One Health, and publish an independent biosafety risk assessment with the final notification.
10. **Publish registration, provenance and inspection data,** including Schedule VI and VII records of animals acquired and traded, so that public-health authorities can trace animal movements in an outbreak.
11. **Rule 4(6) and Rule 11(11): protect the shift to non-animal teaching:** Bar the use of animals for teaching where a validated non-animal alternative meets the learning objective, and cap curriculum-based teaching approvals at three years, with a documented review of alternatives at renewal.
12. **Extend the consultation and align the windows:** Reopen the general public window for at least 60 days, running alongside the window now open to medical institutions, and publish all comments received in both.
